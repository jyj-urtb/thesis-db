Victor BIGNELL, Geoff PETERS and Christopher PYM

The Open University Press, Milton Keynes. 1978, 273 pages

Victor BIGNELL and Joyce FORTUNE

Understanding Systems Failures

Manchester University Press, Manchester, 1984, vii + 216 pages, £4.95

The books Catastrophic Failures and Understanding Systems Failures are reviewed as one set because of the direct correspondence in subject, approach and objective.

Failure is a rather well-known kev-concept in areas such as reliability theory, maintenance theory and risk analysis. Originally failure was defined as a property of a technical system, e.g., in reliability theory. Further development allowed the human element to be included as a source of failure, e.g., in ‘fault tree analysis’ and ‘failure modes, effects and criticality analysis’. Failure as described in the two books is to be understood as catastrophic failure, coming down to a serious, exceptional event catching headlines in the news.

The authors wrote the books to provide written study material for students of the Open University. In their approach they describe a number of real cases, 7 cases in the first book, 5 cases and 6 short studies in the second. The cases are described on the basis of what happened, reconstructing the logical chain or network of events. Complete reference is made to the documents and other resources that were found to be relevant in the exploration and analysis of each case.

Whereas the first book presents analyzed cases only, the second book, in addition to some cases, presents in some 70 final pages considerations aiming at a better understanding of the causes that lead to failure. In these considerations the authors are led by what is known as systems thinking. The two books, therefore, are not two books really, they are rather volume 1 and volume 2 of one message.

There can be no doubt about the merits of the case method as an educational tool if it is applied as in this case: real events, well explored, well documented, presented logically, and last but not least, written like a thriller in an attractive style, without using the ‘easy reading for lazy managers’ language.

Apart from the primary target group, students, the books are of substantial importance to a diversity of scientific disciplines, as well as to a diversity of functions in management and staff of organizations. The material presented clearly demonstrates the limitations of mono-disciplinary models and approaches in the design of man-machine systems of some size. The strong need for a better understanding of catastrophic failure causes is proved regularly; the Bhopal disaster is a recent, sad, example.

The presented material shows that truly foolproof designs can be expected by a fool only. Reduction of failure will require substantial long term efforts of many disciplines combined. Reading this first effort is a must for any one who is interested in failure or failure related matters; even more a must for those that are confident that their monodiscipline primarily has, or could provide, the final answer.
