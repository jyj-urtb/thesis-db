# The relationship of trust and dependence

Colleen E. Patton $^{a}$  and Christopher D. Wickens $^{b}$

$^{a}$Department of Psychology, North Carolina State University, Raleigh, NC, USA; $^{b}$Department of Psychology, Colorado State University, Fort Collins, CO, USA

## ABSTRACT

The concepts of automation trust and dependence have often been viewed as closely related and on occasion, have been conflated in the research community. Yet, trust is a cognitive attitude and dependence is a behavioural measure, so it is unsurprising that different factors can affect the two. Here, we review the literature on the correlation between trust and dependence. On average, this correlation across people was quite low, suggesting that people who are more trusting of automation do not necessarily depend upon it more. Separately, we examined experiments that explicitly manipulated the reliability of automation, finding that higher automation reliability increased trust ratings twice as fast as dependence behaviours. This review provides novel quantitative evidence that the two constructs are not strongly correlated. Implications of this work, including potential moderating variables, contexts where trust is still relevant, and considerations of trust measurement, are discussed.

Practitioner summary: Trust in automation is a cognitive attitude, and dependence on automation is a physical behaviour. Therefore, it is important to understand the differences between the two, especially as they have been conflated in the literature. This review highlights the small average correlation in the literature between subjective trust and objective dependence, which suggests that measuring trust as dependence (or vice versa) may not be valid. This suggests, then, that practitioners should carefully consider how trust and dependence are being measured in a given context so as not to incorrectly conflate the two.

## 1. Introduction

Much of the human-automation literature is focused on understanding when and how human operators decide to use or depend upon automation. This is because ultimately, understanding and predicting how automation changes or impacts tasks with human involvement is crucial for automation designers in real-world scenarios, especially in safety-critical situations such as the implementation of self-driving vehicle technology. Often, human operators drive accuracy of the human-automation team below the accuracy of the automation itself due to incorrectly estimating automation accuracy (Bartlett and McCarley 2018, 2020; Boskemper, Bartlett, and McCarley 2021). This indicates that humans are not depending on the automation in an ideal or optimal way – the reasoning for which human-automation interaction researchers seek to understand. One way that this issue is often investigated is by looking at a person's trust in the automation. The relationship between subjective trust and dependence is of interest for its ability to predict performance, but the strength and direction of this relationship does not seem to be consistent. Here, we define dependence and trust in more detail, and then review the currently available data to show the dissociation between the two constructs.

## 1.1. Operationalising dependence

Simply put, dependence is agreeing with or using automation. It includes both reliance and compliance (Meyer 2004; Meyer and Lee 2013). In automation-supported detection tasks, reliance is when a human agrees with a non-alert or a state of 'all is well' from the automation, regardless of the true state of the task. Reliance is influenced by the frequency of automation misses, where a lack of alerts to true states that require alerts tend to influence operators to show less reliance on the automation (Dixon, Wickens, and McCarley 2007). Reliance is also directly related to complacency, which is when a human operator over-relies on an automated detection system (Bahner, Hüper, and Manzey 2008;

Parasuraman and Manzey 2010). Separately, compliance is defined to occur when an operator agrees with an alert from automation regardless of the true state. We see compliance degraded as false alarms increase, such that automation that sets off a large number of untrue or 'false positive' alerts will lead operators to take longer to respond, if they respond at all (e.g. Dixon and Wickens 2006). These definitions also often apply to diagnostic and decision aids, where the interest is in the extent to which the human's decision agrees either directly with the decision recommended by AI, or indirectly reflects agreement with the AI diagnosis. Dependence can also be defined for non-alerting systems, and in these cases would be operationalised through use of the system. For example, dependence on the automation for a self-driving car can be measured through how often the system is activated and the human is not correcting the automated aspects such as steering or braking.

Dependence (and trust – discussed below) is also measured by eye tracking metrics and visual scanning. Assuming that visual fixations are indicators of attention, metrics such as how often and how long a person looks at the automation can be used to determine their level of dependence on it. For example, Dixon, Wickens, and McCarley (2007) found that automation with many misses (lack of an alert to a true-present target) led to more glances over at the automated task when the automation did not provide an alert, indicating that the operators were less reliant on the automation. This idea is revisited later.

Dependence is of particular interest to practitioners and researchers alike, as the level at which a person depends upon or uses the automation is often predictive of human-automation team performance outcomes, even more so to the extent that the level of performance of the automation exceeds that of the unaided human. Over-dependence or under-dependence, also termed 'misuse' and 'disuse' (Parasuraman and Riley 1997), can lead to poor performance outcomes due to agreement with the automation when it is wrong or disagreement when it is correct. Dependence is sometimes calibrated to the reliability of a system (known as 'probability matching' [Dorfman 1969; Bliss, Gilson, and Deaton 1995]) but often there are still mismatches in correct automation recommendations and correct human responses. This behaviour leads to human-automation team performance at or below the automation's own accuracy levels (Bartlett and McCarley 2018, 2020; Boskemper, Bartlett, and McCarley 2021; Patton et al. 2023). Much research has indicated that one of the strongest influences on dependence is automation reliability or accuracy (Chancey et al., 2017;

Dixon, Wickens, and McCarley 2007; Dixon and Wickens 2006; Hutchinson et al. 2023), such that as automation becomes more reliable, operators use it more. It is also often thought that trust in the automation strongly contributes to dependence.

## 1.2. Trust in automation

Trust as a construct in human-automation interaction evolved from human-human trust (Bainbridge, 1983; Lee and Moray 1992; Hoff and Bashir, 2015). Although the literature in the area is abundant, there is still not a single agreed upon definition (Sheridan 2019). One generally accepted definition is from Lee and See (2004), who define trust as 'the attitude that an agent will help achieve an individual's goals in a situation characterized by uncertainty and vulnerability' [pp. 54]. A full review of trust in automation is outside the scope of this article, and readers are encouraged to visit Hoff and Bashir (2015), Lee and See (2004), or Chiou and Lee (2023) for full reviews. Instead here, a brief summary of the findings of these reviews is provided.

Trust is a dynamic process (Yang, Schemanske, and Searle 2021), it changes over time and across situations. This can be seen in the three main types of trust suggested by Hoff and Bashir (2015): dispositional, situational, and learned. Dispositional trust is an enduring tendency or state of an individual to trust automation regardless of the context. This is typically influenced by factors beyond automation itself, such as culture and age. Situational trust is dependent on the specific context of the interaction with the automation. This can be influenced by external factors – the system, the task it is being used for, the known or perceived reliability of the system, risks and benefits – or by internal factors of the operator such as mood, momentary workload, etc. Lastly, learned trust stems from past experiences with a specific system. This can often change from initial trust (dispositional or situational) and is influenced by a large number of factors such as transparency, performance and predictability of the system, and types of automation errors, including circumstances in which automation does not respond as expected by the human user (Sarter and Woods 1995). Similarly, Schaefer et al. (2016) suggested that trust has three properties: trait, state, and dynamic change. These can be closely tied to dispositional, situational, and learned trust, respectively, from Hoff and Bashir (2015).

Although it is generally accepted that there are different types of trust, measurements of trust vary in their precision and awareness of the types. In practice, trust is measured in many different ways - Kohn et al. (2021) found over 30 measures of trust in their literature review. Measures span self-report surveys, physiological and behavioural measures. The most cited way to measure trust is through the Jian, Bisantz, and Drury (2000) scale, which, through a series of experiments, established a number of questions in a self-report survey designed to capture trust in a specific system. Physiological measures, including neurological measures, heart rate variability, and eye tracking, are also becoming popular in trust measurement. Using eye tracking, the frequency with which an operator looks at the automation or looks at the raw data that the automation is using is assumed to be an indicator of trust, such that more monitoring indicates less trust. This is one example of a behavioural measure that has been used to infer both trust and dependence. Similarly, dependence on the automation is sometimes used as a behavioural measure of trust (e.g. McGuirl and Sarter 2006; Lyons and Stokes 2012; Rice and Geels 2010).

## 1.3. The relationship of trust and dependence

In an early paper discussing trust in automation, Sheridan (1989) suggested that dependence is an attribute of user trust. Specifically, he claimed that depending on automation leads to higher trust because we want to trust the things or people on which we depend. This set an early precedent for the pervasive idea that trust and dependence are closely related, and more recent discussions of trust and dependence sometimes conflate the two, suggesting that dependence can be used to measure trust and vice versa, as if they have a consistently very high correlation.

It appears that this conflation of trust and dependence can be traced back most directly to Meyer's 2001 paper, where he suggests that compliance and reliance are two separate forms of trust. In this experiment, participants were asked to categorise a rectangle by length with the help of a decision aid that made a categorical recommendation. However, 'trust' was measured as their level of agreement with the aid's recommendation. This conflation has been seen in the literature since then, with a number of articles measuring compliance and/or reliance and then pivoting in the discussion to elaborate on 'trust' (see Entin, Entin, and Serfaty 1996; Felbel, Dettmann, and Bullinger 2022; Keller and Rice 2009; Lyons and Stokes 2012; McGuirl and Sarter 2006; Meyer, Wiczorek, and Günzler 2014; Rice 2009; Rice and Geels 2010; Spain and Bliss 2009, for examples).

Additionally, the importance of 'calibrating trust' is often brought up (e.g. Pop, Shrewsbury, and Durso

2015; Wischnewski, Krämer, and Müller 2023). Calibrated trust is a correspondence between a person's trust in a system and the system's capabilities (Lee and See 2004). Although the original definition does not mention dependence, trust calibration is sometimes used as a proxy for calibrated dependence (e.g. McGuirl and Sarter 2006), or is thought to be important because it leads to calibrated dependence. On a surface level, it may make sense that increasing trust will lead to increasing dependence, or vice versa, and this is sometimes true. However, the definition of dependence contains a key piece for measurement: it is a behaviour. Therefore, dependence must be measured based on actions or decisions of a human operator. Similarly, the definition of trust contains a key facet of the concept - the idea of trust as an attitude. It should be quickly clear that the two constructs are not the same and that different variables (e.g. system reliability) may influence each differently. This is often acknowledged by researchers (Lee and See 2004; Merritt et al., 2015), yet the literature explicitly comparing the two and the independent factors that might influence each is limited, and a review of the main findings and implications has not yet been published.

Therefore, this paper reviews the literature to show quantitative evidence that trust and dependence are not strongly correlated. While they are not completely unrelated, they also are not sufficiently correlated to be used as proxies for each other – an idea that, while discussed among scientists frequently, is sometimes ignored (e.g. Felbel, Dettmann, and Bullinger 2022; Keller and Rice 2009; McGuirl and Sarter 2006; Meyer, Wiczorek, and Günzler 2014; Spain and Bliss 2009). Understanding the relationship between the two constructs is crucial for three main reasons. First, if trust and dependence are different metrics, then by using them as proxies for each other, scientists are reducing the validity of their measures. Second, if trust and dependence are closely related, then the predictive value of trust can be explored in future studies, which can provide valuable insight for designers to predict when consumers will use their products. Third, if trust and dependence are not closely related, this opens a discussion around the contexts in which trust should be considered and measured, and where it may be less relevant.

## 1.4. Current review

Lee and See (2004) suggest 'that trust guides, but does not determine, reliance.' Hoff and Bashir (2015) provide the insightful theory that trust and reliance are less related when operators are unable to judge how much the automation is helping them and when there is lower decisional freedom, and suggest that additional situational factors, such as the level of effort required to engage a system, the alternatives to using automation, time constraints, and the operator's situational awareness and physical well-being, can impact dependence without changing trust. Although there is a general agreement that trust and dependence are not the same, this dissociation has not yet been quantified in a review. The measured difference between these two constructs could range across a large scale, and so, without a review of the literature and the effects, the implications of using dependence as a metric for trust, or vice versa, is unclear.

Of specific interest here is also the way that automation reliability influences the trust-dependence relationship. Although previous trust reviews suggest a number of factors that influence dependence and trust, automation reliability is of interest because it is involved in every automation interaction. It is always able to be measured or quantified and often is of interest in real world environments. Therefore, its influence on the trust-dependence relationship should be considered.

The current review aims to provide a quantification of the way in which subjective trust and objective dependence are related based on published correlations between the two variables. From published papers regarding trust, dependence, compliance, and reliance in the Human Factors field, the reported correlations of participant trust and dependence were collected and averaged to understand the relationship. Effect sizes for changes in trust and dependence across automation reliability conditions were also collected. It was hypothesised (H1) that trust and dependence overall would have a small positive correlation due to most studies reporting significant yet small or moderate correlations, as is often seen in published psychological literature. Further, it was hypothesised (H2) that within an experiment, when reliability is increased between conditions, although both trust and dependence would increase, the amount of increase between the two dependent variables would not strongly correspond. H1 addresses the relationship between dispositional trust and dependence, whereas H2 addresses the relationship between situational trust and dependence. Lastly, it was hypothesised (H3) that when dependence was measured through visual scanning patterns (rather than reliance, compliance or use), there would be a weak negative correlation between the amount of looking at the automation and the rating of trust.

## 2. Methods

Published, peer-reviewed articles were collected and reviewed for eligibility. Articles were collected from Google Scholar, the Human Factors Journal, the Human Factors and Ergonomics Conference Annual Proceedings, Computers in Human Behaviour Journal, Ergonomics in Design, and a university database (Summons). Search terms for the journals included 'trust + dependence', 'trust + compliance' and 'trust + reliance', and 'trust + eye tracking'. These search terms were then repeated with the addition of '+ reliability'. In Google Scholar and the university database, these search terms had 'automation' added so that human-human trust would not be included. This allowed an encompassing search of papers that covered trust and dependence generally, as well as with automation reliability manipulations.

This resulted in 142 articles that appeared relevant based on titles or abstracts. Articles were then reviewed in detail to ensure they measured and reported both subjective trust and objective dependence. Articles that were deemed relevant but did not report the necessary measures for inclusion in the analyses are listed in Appendix B. Subjective trust could be measured through any type of questionnaire and at any point in a study. Although the differentiation between types of trust is covered in review papers, few experimental papers specify the type of trust they measured and therefore, those that did specify the type of trust were included as separate measures of trust but were not correlated any differently. Dependence could be measured as automation use (turning the automation on) or agreement with the automation. Importantly, dependence could not be gathered from accuracy reports and thus papers that did not report reliance, compliance or use as a form of dependence were excluded, alongside any articles that did not measure subjective trust. Eye tracking metrics, such as monitoring, were often reported in articles but can be assumed to be fundamentally different from behavioural forms of dependence and thus were included separately from the reliance/compliance papers. Appendix D lists the reviewed articles and their trust and dependence measures more specifically.

Additionally, studies that manipulated reliability typically reported trust and dependence separately, so specific correlations could not be gathered, but general trends could be gleaned. To examine the extent to which trust and dependence co-varied between reliability conditions, a different approach to analysis was adopted. From the papers gathered initially, those that (a) manipulated automation reliability between two or more levels and (b) reported an effect size or statistics to calculate an effect size for trust and dependence across the manipulated levels were kept for analysis. In these articles, we inferred that a precisely equal effect size for both trust and dependence would, in this co-variation analysis, correspond to a correlation of 1.0 in the between-people weighted correlational analysis. Any situation of a 0-effect size for one or the other measure would correspond to a correlation $r = 0$. Between these two extremes, the ratio of the two effect sizes (here expressed as $[d_{\mathrm{trust}} / d_{\mathrm{dependence}}]$) would establish the extent to which differences in trust would also be reflected in differences in dependence.

This created three categories of articles (see Appendix A for a more detailed reference list) – trust and behavioural dependence (18 articles), trust and eye tracking metrics (12 articles), and trust and dependence with a reliability manipulation (10 articles). Where possible, direct correlations between subjective trust and objective dependence were gathered. These were used to calculate a weighted average correlation.

## 3. Results

Two classes of data are reviewed here. The first encompasses correlations (such as Pearson's r) between subjective measures of trust and behavioural metrics of dependence. The second class of data is focused on what we refer to as co-variance data, where summary statistics for trust and dependence across different experimental groups or conditions are used to understand the impact of automation reliability on the difference in changes between trust and dependence. The co-variance data provides insight into the relationship between trust and dependence across conditions (i.e. emphasising situational trust).

## 3.1. Correlational studies

Correlations that were reported between participants for subjective trust and objective dependence were averaged by weight. Objective dependence was considered any behavioural measure of reliance, compliance, or use. For automation use, accepted measures included turning the automation on or off, including manual behaviours or interventions. When the behaviour was a manual takeover or turning the automation off, the correlation was inverted, under the assumption that manual interventions were the opposite of dependence. The correlations and associated studies are provided in Table 1. An average weighted correlation between trust and dependence was computed, resulting in a mean r-value of +.11. The range of correlations was from -.24 up to .77, with a standard deviation of .26. This small correlation would suggest that, on average, trust accounts for only about 1% of the variance in dependence across these studies. However, it is important to note that this small average may be misleading in some cases and therefore, a more thorough breakdown of trends is necessary.

From the available data, it appears that, for detection studies, trust correlates more strongly with measures of reliance ( $r_{w} = .14$ ) than measures of compliance ( $r_{w} = .02$ ). This correlation also appears higher than that of automation use and trust ( $r_{w} = .02$ ). This suggests that agreeing with the automation when it suggests that the state of the world is 'all is well' is more closely related to trust than agreeing with the automation when it suggests that there is an issue that needs addressing. Additionally, single question scales of trust created by the authors – which typically were phrased something like 'how much do you trust this system' – were more strongly correlated with dependence measures ( $r_{w} = .11$ ) than the Jian, Bisantz, and Drury (2000) trust scale ( $r_{w} = .05$ ). This is surprising, as Jian, Bisantz, and Drury (2000) is generally considered a more valid measure, but these numbers should be interpreted with caution, as there are only a few correlations contributing to the averages and there could be other factors contributing to the low correlations from measurement or study design due to inherent differences in the studies that use these different types of measures.

Interestingly, the places where we see the highest correlations have no clear similarities between them, nor clear differences from the studies with lower correlations. Post-hoc, it was thought that studies where manual performance was high would lead to stronger correlations. However, very few studies that reported the trust and dependence correlations also reported manual (or even human-automated team) performance. The two highest correlations did come from Muir and Moray (1996) and a replication study (Lee et al. 2021), where performance was near the ceiling, which provides a small amount of evidence for the idea that the trust-dependence relationship may be somewhat dependent on the level of unaided task performance.

Additionally, it does not appear that the degree of automation impacts the correlation. The highest correlations from Muir and Moray (1996) and Lee et al. (2021, 2023) were high levels of automation where the automation performed the task, but the other top five strongest correlations were lower degrees of automation that only provided recommendations for a decision.

At this point, it seems that trust and dependence behaviours are not clearly correlated. However, if we look to the literature on monitoring behaviour, as measured by eye tracking, the story is slightly different.

## 3.1.1. Trust and monitoring

The literature that reported correlations between subjective trust and scan-related monitoring of the automation appeared fundamentally different from the trust and dependence literature. This is because monitoring is not a true behavioural measure that influences performance in the same way as the choice to use automation in reaching a decision. Therefore, these correlations were averaged separately. Monitoring was typically captured on either the automated task or the non-automated task (in driving research, often labelled the non-driving-related task). In this case, the correlation of the monitoring of the non-automated tasks was multiplied by -1, under the assumption that it represented the inverse of monitoring the driving task. Across thirteen studies (Table 2), the average weighted correlation came out to be r=-.43, suggesting that trust accounts for about 18% of the variation in monitoring. Put simply, people who trust automation more, look at it less often.

However, the assumption that monitoring the non-automated task is the inverse of monitoring the automated task may not always be true and thus, a secondary analysis was conducted to find the weighted correlation of monitoring on the automated task and on the non-automated task. Results indicated that for monitoring of the automated task, $r_w = -.41$. Monitoring of the non-automated task showed a correlation with trust of $r_w = .57$. In other words, when people trust the automation more, they look at the non-automated task more often and the automated task less often – particularly in driving.

## 3.2. Co-variance studies

The previous analysis suggested that there was very little shared variance across people between trust and dependence. That is, the data of the correlational analyses suggested that individual differences in trust do not effectively predict how much a person will actually depend on automation in a given human-automation team interaction. However, a somewhat different picture appears to emerge when we examine the co-variance between trust and dependence across conditions (rather than people) that vary in automation reliability. Because there were fewer such studies than the correlational studies, these are each summarised individually as follows, in which we have endeavoured to extract from each, some measure of effect size that is reported and is the same for each of the two variables, so that common units can be compared as a ratio. The extracted effect sizes for each paper are reported in Table 3.

When feedback was displayed immediately (Experiment 1), for dependence, Cohen's d=3 and for trust, d=0.89. When feedback was both delayed and expressed by the robot in an 'emphatic format' (Experiment 3), the dependence effect size was d=0.33 and for trust, d=3.1.

Du, Huang, and Yang (2020) used a threat detection task and with an automated threat detector that was either highly reliable (response criterion of -.025 and sensitivity measure d' of 3) or less reliable (criterion of -.025 and sensitivity of 1.5). Trust and reliability were less equivalently affected, with a ratio of F-values between the two dependent variables of 7.53/5.90.

Elder et al. (2022) examined performance in a basketball odds-making game involving betting on the predicted winner of professional NBA teams. An automation aid offered advice, at reliability levels of 0.56 and 0.82. For dependence, averaged over reliance and compliance, the F-value was 6.75. No measure of situational trust was reported, but for perceived reliability, a proxy of trust given at each reliability level, the F-value was 60. The investigators also measured dispositional trust, but as this is a measure of the person, not the effect of condition, it was not included.

Fox and Boehm-Davis (1998) examined driving navigation performance when a decision aid that recommended a direction of turn to avoid traffic had reliabilities of 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0. The effects of reliability were relatively linear, so we employed the F value of the ANOVA conducted across the entire range (i.e. including R=0.40). They found an effect of dependence (F=16) and trust (F=105).

Madhavan and Phillips (2010) employed an automation-aided luggage screening task at reliability levels of 0.70 and 0.90. Participants always viewed the image followed by the automation advice, before making their decision. Their statistics revealed that for dependence F=4.7 and for trust, F=8.8.

Rieger and Manzey (2022) examined automation's support for weapons detection in a baggage screening task at 95% and 75% reliability. In Experiment 1, when automation's advice was offered before participants made their tentative judgement, the dependence eta squared was 0.93 (averaged over both reliance and compliance). For trust, using a different statistic, the effect size was d=0.88. Neither of these effects was significant. In Experiment 2, when the automation offered its advice after the human's initial judgement (as in Madhaven and Phillips), the effect of reliability on dependence had an effect size of  $\eta^{2}=0.10$ , and for trust, d=0.52.

Sanchez, Fisk, and Rogers (2004) varied the reliability of an automation monitoring aid across three levels – 60%, 80% and 100%. They measured trust on a six-point scale and dependence as an objective metric ranging from 0% to 100%. Since actual effect sizes were not reported, the degree of shared variance across the three levels was estimated by the ratio of the reported ANOVA F-values. This ratio of F scores was 61.0 (trust)/20.4 (dependence).

Strickland et al. (2023) examined human interaction with an air traffic control conflict detection aid that varied between 60% and 95% reliability levels. Although they did not report F statistics, from the standard error data reported in the authors' data table, we could estimate the effect size on measured trust between the two reliability conditions (mean = 2.32 and 3.58, a difference of 10 SEs) and dependence (52% to 79%; a difference of 4.25 SEs).

In considering the data within the table, it is important to note that every study showed an increase in trust and dependence as reliability increased (although both were not always significant), thus signalling the general co-variance between the two variables. However, the specific values within the table reveal two further important findings.

The first finding revealed by the table is the direction of reliability-caused difference between dependence and trust of effect sizes across studies. Of the 12 experiments reported, only two (those with trust and dependence values italicized) showed a pattern of differences such that the increase in dependence with increasing reliability was greater than the increase in trust. A test of binomial proportions revealed that the difference between 2/12 and 6/12 (equality: the null hypothesis) is significant (p<.05). That is, the proportion 2/12 (0.17) is more than 2 standard errors below 6/12 (0.50).

The second finding concerns the difference in the magnitude of the increase of dependence versus trust as reliability increases, and this becomes somewhat more difficult to interpret because these measures tend to be skewed. In particular, any effect size based on a squared value, such as an F test or an eta-squared measure, will show a positively skewed distribution of either trust or dependence. To adjust for this, in the two 'transformed' columns on the right of the table, we have applied a square root transformation to each of these co-variance measures within the squared subset (i.e. F and eta-squared). These are bold-faced. The non-squared measures (e.g. d) are not. As shown in the mean transformed values at the bottom row of the table, the effect of increasing reliability on trust (3.47) is nearly twice the size of the effect on dependence (1.81), which is a significant difference, even with such small degrees of freedom  $t(12) = -2.54, p = .02, d = 0.67$ .

## 4. Discussion

This review set out to understand the extent to which trust in and dependence on automation are related. While many researchers will agree that trust and dependence are not the same, the list of published studies that use dependence as a measure of trust is non-trivial (e.g., Entin, Entin, and Serfaty 1996; McGuirl and Sarter 2006; Lyons and Stokes 2012; Rice and Geels 2010; Rice 2009; Keller and Rice 2009; Spain and Bliss 2009; Meyer, Wiczorek, and Günzler 2014), indicating that this generalisation is not always upheld in experimental design. Importantly, understanding the extent of the relationship between trust and dependence can speak to the validity of experiments that have measured them using the same metric. Beyond the basic science contributions, the relationship also is important to define because it can help practitioners understand when they should care about user trust for their products or systems.

The results of this review indicated that, as expected (hypothesis 1), trust and dependence - across individuals and when trust is considered in a more dispositional form (Hoff and Bashir 2015) - are not strongly correlated. That is, those who trust automation more, do not necessarily use it more (average weighted $r = +.11$). This suggests that using dependence to measure trust is unlikely to provide an accurate measure of subjective trust, and vice versa. Instead, there must be other factors influencing dependence outside of trust that explain additional variance. The range of correlations here was quite large, but no clear patterns emerged surrounding the potential moderating variable of degree of automation nor manual performance on the task, although the latter lacked enough data from the studies collected to make a clear determination. These results suggest that the variables impacting subjectively rated trust are far from identical to those that impact compliance, reliance, or use.

This brings up some important considerations. Intuitively, it makes sense that as a person's trust in automation increases, their dependence should increase as well. In certain overload situations, this may be seen less often, as reliance on the automation may be a necessity to complete a task, regardless of a person's feelings towards the automation. However, in more general situations, where manual (unaided) task performance can be at an acceptable level, it would seem against intuition that the relationship would be so small. There are two potential reasons for this:

First, a recent paper theorises that trust in automation is a result of the ability to predict the future performance of the automation (Carter, Loft, and Visser 2024). If this is the case, then trust can be high when dependence is low, because if the operator can predict poor performance from the automation, they may trust it without depending on it. This would also explain the increase in trust and slower decrease in dependence as reliability increased – knowing the automation will likely perform well can improve trust, but does not inherently change dependence, if the operators can predict when it will fail.

The second reason is the way that subjective trust (surveys) is measured is either not entirely valid, or the wide range of ways used to measure trust means that more valid measures are offset by less valid measures. It is unlikely that the former is true, considering the low correlation between dependence and trust as measured by the Jian, Bisantz, and Drury (2000) scale, which was created from experiments of people's rankings of words related to trust. However, it is possible that other measures of trust, especially scenario-specific, single questions created by authors, could be less valid and reduce this correlation. The variations in timepoints and number of surveys administered between studies could also be contributing to the low correlation. Additionally, in the correlations reported here, people's ratings of trust are based on their dispositional trust. Yet almost by definition, the measure of dependence must reflect a situational influence (Hoff and Bashir 2015). The data reported in the next section indicates that the co-variation between dependence and situational trust, across different reliability conditions is somewhat higher, although still far less than the maximum possible. It is also quite likely, of course, that there are other factors influencing the choice to depend on automation beyond trust – these are detailed in the following section.

## 4.1. Co-variance in automation reliability

Automation reliability showed a consistent trend such that both trust and dependence increased as reliability increased. Although again this trend was not able to be precisely quantified, our estimation indicated that as reliability increased, the increases in trust were at least twice as great as the increases in dependence. This provides some support for hypothesis two (H2), such that the increase between the two variables does not strongly correspond, but we do see a general relationship in the doubling of trust increases. The trend here, in contrast to the small average correlation from studies of individual trust-dependence relationships, may be a result of a change in the type of trust that is being measured by individuals as compared to across environmental contexts.

The consistency across these few studies that compared reliability conditions rather than comparing people may be responsible, in part, for the pervasive idea that trust can be measured through dependence. However, it is important to note that in these cases, while trust and dependence generally increase in tandem across conditions of increasing reliability (Table 3), they are not the same metric. Rather, these studies suggest that if an experiment manipulates automation reliability and sees a change in dependence (or trust), it may be assumed that trust (or dependence) is changing in that same direction – but the amount of change will be quite different between the two variables: the volatility or sensitivity of subjective trust is considerably greater than that of actual behaviour.

Although automation reliability provides some insight into influencing factors, there are of course other factors not addressed here that must influence the trust-dependence relationship. Some literature has suggested that self-confidence of the operator in completing the task manually influences dependence or use (Lee and Moray 1992; Riley 1995; Merritt 2011; Patton 2023), and the interaction of self-confidence with trust is a better predictor than trust alone. There are also environmental factors that may influence dependence such as risk, mental workload, or mental overhead (Parasuraman & Riley, 1997). Hoff and Bashir (2015) cover many other influences in detail, differentiating between influences of the human operator, the environment, and the automated system. Reliability is a system variable, but the human and environmental variables and many other factors from the system have not been researched in enough detail to provide an understanding of when trust and dependence are strongly correlated.

## 4.2. Trust and monitoring

We analysed monitoring studies separately because where and how long a person looks at an automated system, or displays of the raw data processed by automation, can be considered to be both a measure of trust (you look less because you trust it to do its job more) and dependence (you look more because you need its advice to make a diagnostic response). This hybrid status of monitoring, and the third hypothesis of a negative correlation between the two, is confirmed because trust and monitoring were much more strongly correlated (r=-.43) than subjective trust and dependence. This was true for monitoring the automation/automated task (r=-.41), and for monitoring the non-automated task (r=.57). These findings indicate that subjective trust and looking are related – more trust in the automation leads to looking at it less often and looking at the non-automated task more often in dual-task scenarios. This finding has implications for understanding when designers and practitioners should care about trust. If a user or operator will look at a non-automated task more often when they trust the automation, performance is also likely to increase on that task because of the additional attention allocated to it (Dixon, Wickens, and McCarley 2007). Therefore, if the non-automated task is a priority, it is important to ensure that there is an adequate level of trust in the automation so that it does not have to be monitored as closely and more resources can be dedicated to the primary task.

Additionally, the relationship between monitoring and trust is noteworthy because of the need for a dynamic measure of trust. Subjective trust is often measured through questionnaires that are presented at the end of an experiment, or throughout an experiment by pausing the trial and presenting the questions. Researchers have suggested that these may not capture the way trust can change moment to moment (Yang, Schemanske, and Searle 2021), but monitoring of the automation may provide a non-intrusive way to measure trust over time. With only a moderate correlation there is still room for changes in trust that are not caught through monitoring, but it is a step towards better capturing trust changes.

## 4.3. Reliance and compliance vs monitoring

However, it is important to note that dependence and monitoring are still not the same construct, as monitoring is a low effort engagement requiring only eye movements whereas dependence requires an active choice to agree with the automation's recommendation or turn the automation on. It is possible that the difference in the correlations between these two constructs and trust may be due, in part, to this difference in effort level.

Similarly, reliance on the automation was seen to have a higher correlation (r=.14) with trust than compliance on the automation (r=.02). Reliance requires less effort on the part of the operator, as it is an agreement with the automation that 'all is well' or no input is needed. Compliance requires more effort, as it is a response to an alert from the automation indicating some action needs to be taken. Both correlations are low and thus should be interpreted with caution, but this provides an additional hint that when more effort is needed, trust is less likely to be related to the behavioural outcome. Similarly, automation use, which might require the most effort as it is an active choice to use automation or, in the case of a few studies here, intervening to take over from the automation, was not strongly correlated with trust either. None of the experiments reviewed here made explicit note of how difficult engaging or disengaging the automation was, but this creates an avenue for future research to pursue.

## 4.3. Future directions

This literature review makes clear a need for four main considerations in the future:

1. There is a need for reporting subjective trust and objective dependence measures more often, especially if claims are made about both metrics. The literature on trust and dependence individually is vast, but the number of studies that provide results of both is limited. Therefore, the importance of studies that investigate trust and dependence correlations should not be overlooked. These findings should also be reported in papers that may not be solely focused on the relationship but have measured both outcomes for other purposes.

2. Relatedly, there is a need to report the results of studies in ways that allow for meta-analyses and reviews to be conducted. While this is not limited to the trust and dependence literature, it is one area that makes clear the benefit of reporting means, standard deviations, and, in particular, effect sizes for all effects, regardless of significance, in an empirical research paper.

3. Systematic, empirical investigations are needed to better understand the mechanisms through which trust and dependence are related so that scientists and practitioners can better predict trust and dependence outcomes. More research is needed to understand the impact of variables such as task performance, training, risk, degree of automation, and perceptions of the context or automation. This will allow real-world performance to be predicted, and the theoretical understanding of the dissociation between trust and dependence to be improved.

4. The idea that 'trust calibration' is a key piece of human-automation interaction should be considered carefully. Trust calibration is a term that refers to the idea that an operator's trust aligns with the reliability of the automation (Lee and See 2004), and implicitly suggests that their dependence or use of the automation will be at the proper level. However, when trust and dependence are not strongly correlated, calibration of trust and dependence should be considered separately. Trust should be calibrated to reduce the negative impact on the operator's experience or perception of the automation and task - appropriate trust can improve the workload experience of the operator, thereby reducing their stress. Dependence should be calibrated to improve human-automation team performance. These two constructs can be addressed based on the extent of their importance in a given environment.

## 4.4. Limitations

The current review has two main limitations worth noting. First, a number of articles were found during the review process that discussed both subjective trust and dependence but did not provide the necessary numbers or statistics to be included in the current paper. These papers are listed in Appendix B for reference by readers. Second, some papers, such as Wiczorek and Meyer (2019), report only significant correlations or effects and then mention non-significant effects, but do not report the needed numbers for a review. Both of these limitations, due in part to publication bias, can skew the results of the current review, likely such that the average correlations reported here are larger than the ground truth. There is scope here to conduct a true meta-analysis of the work on trust and dependence that includes unpublished data, but the goal of the current article was to review the published work and set a foundation for future research in this area.

## 4.5. Conclusions

As expected, trust and dependence were not strongly correlated, but trust and monitoring did see a stronger correlation. The impact of increasing automation reliability on trust was almost twice that of dependence, providing further support for the dissociation between the two constructs. This is not to say that trust and dependence are entirely independent of each other, but rather they need to be considered in tandem, instead of as substitutions for each other in measurement. It is likely that their relationship is moderated by other variables such as the effort required in engaging with the automation or manual performance on the task – but further research is needed to understand these effects.

It is important to note that the lack of relationship between trust and dependence does not implicate that trust does not matter. Rather, it suggests that trust should be considered in its own right. Muir & Moray (1996) suggest that trust matters because it impacts the perceptions of the operator. An operator who does not trust a system but must depend it on for the sake of performance is likely to feel discomfort or stress from the cognitive dissonance. Operator perceptions are important independent of dependence because they can lead to dissatisfaction in a job or with a system.

The current review also brings up a need to carefully consider the way that trust is measured. Whether single question surveys developed by authors versus empirically derived surveys (i.e. Jian, Bisantz, and Drury 2000) are better than the other is beyond the scope of the current results, but it does bring an important consideration to light. Trust is a difficult concept to operationalise because it is an attitude (Lee and See 2004). Therefore, it may be beneficial to determine why trust is relevant – is there a need to predict dependence, or the intent to rely on a system? Is the focus on comfort or perceptions of the operator? By focusing on the reason that trust is of interest, the best way to measure it – or not measure it – can be determined. Care must be taken by researchers to determine the context in which the two constructs are being measured and the relative importance of each in the applications of the research. Both trust and dependence have implications for design and use of automation through their impacts on performance and perception of the human operator. Yet, by measuring one as the other, the important differences between the constructs are lost.

## Appendix C. Additional details on reported statistics from Tables 1

Elder et al. (2022) explicitly reported a measure of dispositional trust with reliance and compliance.

Merritt and Ilgen (2008) reported both initial trust (r = .31) and post-task trust (r = .33). They also reported propensity to trust (r = .14) but that was not included in the current analyses because it is unclear if propensity to trust is the same as current trust.

Wang, Jamieson, and Hollands (2011) reported the correlation coefficient tau ( $\tau$ ) so this was converted to Pearson's r using Gilpin's (1993) table.

Lee and Moray (1992) reported the amount of variance accounted for by three pumps. The square root of these three variances (5.3%, 3.4% and 3.1%) were taken to calculate the correlation coefficient. The correlations were then averaged into one, which is a reasonable approach because there appeared to be system-wide trust in the three pumps and all numbers came from the same experiment.

Lee et al. (2023), and Tenhundfeld et al. (2020) reported manual use or manual takeovers, so the inverse of the correlations was reported here. For example, Tenhundfeld et al. (2020) reported a correlation of -.393, so here it was reported as .393.
