## Summary

Multitasking—the performance of several tasks at the same time—is becoming increasingly prevalent in workplaces. Multitasking is known to disrupt performance, particularly in complex and dynamic situations, which is exactly what most military occupations entail. Because military errors can be consequential, the detrimental impact of multitasking on cognitive functioning in such contexts should be taken seriously. This review pertains to high‐consequence military occupations that require strong multitasking skills. More specifically, it highlights cognitive challenges arising from different forms of multitasking and discusses their underlying cognitive processes. Because such challenges are not expected to diminish, this review proposes context‐relevant solutions to decrease occupational workload, either by reducing the cognitive load ensuing from the to‐be‐performed tasks or by improving soldiers' multitasking abilities. To ensure effective implementation of these solutions, we stress the need to design context‐adapted tools and procedures, and to guide human resource managers in developing particular strategies.

cognition, military personnel, multitasking, performance, workload

## 1 | INTRODUCTION

The continued presence of technologies in our public and private lives is undoubtedly changing many aspects of our functioning within these spheres, including the management of daily tasks. Some argue that one benefit of accessible technologies (smart phones, tablets, and computers) is our ability to manage multiple tasks simultaneously, or multitasking (Loukopoulos, Dismukes, & Barshi, 2009). Because the involved tasks compete for our attention, multitasking typically results in a decline in each task's performance (Hodgetts & Jones, 2006; Monsell, 2003). Highly complex and dynamic situations that require individuals to engage in cognitive processing and decision making or to keep pace with or anticipate rapid environmental changes are not limited to our personal lives. Indeed, the multitasking of cognitively‐complex tasks is characteristic of many occupational fields, leading many to question its effects on employee productivity and errors (e.g., Kinney, 2007; Rottapel, 2018; Russ & Crews, 2014).

The consequences of employee errors resulting from multitasking can be critical or even life threatening in certain occupations, like the military whose main goal is safeguarding state interests and civilian security. Many military occupations require soldiers to manage several complex tasks concurrently (see Pew & Mavor, 1998). Major General Gorden, when he served as the Director of Military Personnel Management in the U.S. Army, explained that “We expect a much more complex, dynamic, and uncertain environment in the future. Selecting those who can handle uncertainty may be the greatest challenge that we have” (Gorden, 1994, p. x). Military personnel may be tempted to manage the uncertainty in their work and environment by multitasking. Yet, experience is not always sufficient to avoid committing critical errors in these contexts. Indeed, even experts put in highly demanding situations can make inappropriate and dangerous decisions (Dehais et al., 2014). However, the ability to multitask can be improved with training (Dux et al., 2009; Walker et al., 2010). Moreover, correlates of multitasking ability have been identified including polychronicity (i.e., preferences and attitudes associated with multitasking; Kantrowitz, Grelle, Beaty, & Wolf, 2012), as well as attentional control and goal maintenance (Kane & Engle, 2002; Watson, Lambert, Miller, & Strayer, 2011), suggesting that they could improve one's multitasking ability. However, the bulk of the literature suggests that multitasking has its limitations, and that even individuals who are deemed multitasking efficient encounter difficulties in contexts in which several tasks must be carried out simultaneously (Sanbonmatsu, Strayer, Medeiros‐Ward, & Watson, 2013).

The purpose of this article is twofold. First, we review the current literature on multitasking and military occupations known to require strong multitasking skills. Specifically, taking a cognitive approach, we discuss the impacts of military multitasking on attentional resources and memory functioning, and how these cognitive effects manifest in soldiers' performance in specific military occupations. We chose to illustrate these issues through the key domains of piloting, command and control (C2), and ground combat occupations, but one must keep in mind that the principles addressed here also apply to other military and even nonmilitary occupations. Second, in addition to our discussion of potential limitations from the presence of multi-tasking in military occupations, we also discuss potential solutions for the reduction of multitasking‐related cognitive deficits in domainspecific contexts. Although the focus of the current review is the military domain, the considerations discussed here are of additional relevance for certain nonmilitary safety‐critical work domains characterized by the need to attend to multiple tasks in complex, dynamic environments, such as emergency medicine (Croskerry, 2000), urban security surveillance (Hodgetts, Vachon, Chamberland, & Tremblay, 2017), and emergency response (Gasaway, 2013).

## 2 | GENERAL OVERVIEW OF MULTITASKING: TYPES, MECHANISMS, AND OUTCOMES

There are different forms of multitasking, depending on the types of tasks involved and the manner in which they are performed: dual tasking, task switching, and task interruption. Dual tasking is the performance of more than one task simultaneously (Lee & Taatgen, 2002). For example, when scanning the environment for targets a tank gunner must simultaneously identify targets, operate their gun, and all while listening to the tank commander's instructions. Task switching involves alternating one's attention from one task to another when executing two or more tasks (Monsell, 2003). A drone operator, for instance, must repeatedly command the drone to particular positions and locations, and coordinate tactical moves with the rest of the infantry group. Finally, task interruption is characterized by the temporary suspension of an ongoing task, triggered by the requirement to execute a higher priority task (Hodgetts & Jones, 2006). For example, a surveillance operator must suspend a task when asked by a superior for details on a situation. Research demonstrates that all three forms of multitasking are related to deficits in cognitive performance; however, the cognitive mechanisms by which they produce those deficits are different.

In dual tasking, declines in cognitive performance stem from limitations in attentional resources. When managing two tasks simultaneously, capacity‐limited attentional resources are shared between tasks, which results in cognitive overload when cognitive capacity limits are reached or exceeded by task demands (Jaeggi et al., 2007). When this occurs, performance is disrupted whether resources are more general (Strayer & Johnston, 2001), or specific to the implied modalities (Wickens, 2002). On the other hand, with task switching, decreases in cognitive performance are mainly caused by frequent changes in one's cognitive framework. When one switches attention to a new task, the appropriate procedural schema and configuration of cognitive resources must be established to execute the new task optimally. Some authors have suggested that switch costs arise from the need to overcome the previous (automatic) inhibition of the relevant task set (Allport, Styles, & Hsieh, 1994), while others have suggested that they result from the reconfiguration of the cognitive system by executive control processes (Rogers & Monsell, 1995). Regardless of the mechanisms, task switching is particularly detrimental to performance, even when individuals are in control of when the switch takes place (Arrington & Logan, 2004). With task interruption, cognitive deficits arise from the retrieval of goals related to the interrupted task (Altmann & Trafton, 2002). Once the interruption is over and the main task needs to be resumed, the main task goals must be retrieved from memory and reactivated. These task‐resumption operations require cognitive efforts that tend to temporarily impede the execution of the primary task. In complex and dynamic situations typical of military operations, dealing with task interruptions is particularly challenging, given that situations can continue to evolve during the interruption. Indeed, an operator attempting to resume an interrupted dynamic task must not only remember the goals and state of completion of that task but also identify any real or potential changes that might have taken place during the interruption and evaluate their impact (Hodgetts, Vachon, & Tremblay, 2014; St. John & Smallman, 2008). Operators must then regain situational awareness: perceiving stimuli in one's environment, evaluating their significance, and predicting how they can evolve (Endsley, 1988), all of which demand cognitive resources.

Although different forms of multitasking involve different mechanisms, they all disrupt the execution of cognitive processes and impede cognitive performance. According to Thomas and Russo (2007), 80% to 85% of military accidents are caused by human errors resulting from failures of cognitive performance. Given that multitasking interferes with cognitive processing, it is important to better understand how military accidents might be attributed to multitasking, in order to propose appropriate solutions. This issue is particularly relevant because many tasks in military operations tend to be complex, involving many variables linked to one another in complex ways (Bonner, 1994), with research indicating that multitasking has a greater impact on complex tasks relative to simpler ones (Speier, Vessey, & Valacich, 2003).

## 3 | MULTITASKING IN MILITARY OCCUPATIONS

Despite advances in technology, the success of military missions is largely dependent on the human agent. To maximize the effectiveness of military personnel in multitasking contexts, it is crucial to understand the challenges affecting occupations requiring the performance of multiple critical tasks. In this section, we examine the military occupations of piloting, C2, and ground combat where cognitive deficits similar to those described in the previous section could originate from multitasking behaviors.

## 3.1 | Piloting

Air travel is remarkably safe, but accidents do occur in civilian and military aviation. Between 1987 and 2001, there were 27 major U.S. airline accidents in which crew error was found to be a causal factor, with most of them linked to experiences of cognitive overload (Dismukes, 2007). Pilots are required to schedule and execute multiple tasks, while at least monitoring their statuses concurrently, even if the specific tasks are not executed simultaneously. These tasks include testing the preflight system, configuring the initial flight, landing and powering down the aircraft, programming the flight management computer, controlling the movement of the aircraft, and navigating toward a specific destination aided by signals from different sources, while also ensuring that the flight crew's tasks are completed. Pilots must switch attention among concurrent tasks, being careful not to neglect tasks not currently attended to. Indeed, accident reports indicate that mistakes are more likely to happen when pilots try to manage or monitor several tasks at the same time (Dismukes, 2007).

According to Loukopoulos et al. (2009), operating an aircraft requires the use of three main cognitive processes: prospective memory, automatic processing, and switching attention between tasks. These are not the only cognitive processes, but the main processes involved. Pilot errors typically result from (a) interruptions or distractions; (b) encountering tasks needed to be performed outside of the normal task sequence; (c) new tasks arising; or (d) switching between multiple tasks. Naturally, pilots cannot always suspend one task until another has been completed. Instead, they often must shift or divide their attention among tasks. Pilots exposed to multitasking of this type or to new situations tend to commit more errors, sometimes unknowingly, even when they have extensive experience (Loukopoulos et al., 2009). Many such errors tend to be the result of attentional tunneling, an excessive attentional focus on a specific channel of information irrelevant to the task goal (Wickens & Alexander, 2009). Attentional tunneling is largely due to a difficulty in suppressing a task following a temporary reduction in cognitive resources, typically brought on by an intense emotional response or stress (Simpson, Drevets, Snyder, Gusnard, & Raichle, 2001). This form of tunneling can lead to inattentional deafness, which can result in the failure of pilots to detect an alert that they would notice under typical conditions of decreased cognitive load (Dalton & Fraenkel, 2012; Dehais et al., 2014).

The automatic execution of certain tasks may also increase the likelihood of pilot error. When experienced pilots perform a routine procedure, they usually do it with less effort and fewer errors. However, pilots must approach certain tasks with more deliberate or conscious processing such as when learning new tasks, engaging in critical or hazardous tasks, encountering unusual situations, or having to choose among competing tasks or objectives. These more deliberate tasks tend to be carried out slower, requiring greater effort, and must be performed in sequence (Barshi & Healy, 1993). The different cognitive skills required to pilot an aircraft are sensitive to the number of tasks performed, which explains the increase in errors committed when pilots are required to undertake new tasks or engage in multitasking. In other words, the likelihood of committing critical errors likely increases when pilots are confronted with multiple concurrent tasks, where even experience may not buffer against the detrimental cognitive effects of multitasking.

## 3.2 | Command and control

C2 occupations include emergency management, police or firefighting operations, and radar surveillance, and all entail providing information, orders, and directions to be used by others. In addition, C2 operators engage in critical decision making, and the completion of multiple goals in complex, dynamic, uncertain, and stressful environments (Grier, 2015). The strategic and tactical decisions required in these fields tend to be made rapidly and using several sources of information (Rovira, McGarry, & Parasuraman, 2007). They may require the execution of concurrent or alternating tasks and expose C2 operators to information overload (Loft, 2014). Soldiers in these domains are responsible for the outcomes of military operations by providing orders and instructions, coordinating activities, and ensuring that the right resources are properly deployed (Brehmer, 2007). Air traffic control (ATC), for example, involves monitoring large numbers of aircraft, arriving from different directions, at differing speeds and altitudes, and departing in different directions (Gronlund, Ohrt, Dougherty, Perry, & Manning, 1998). Some of the skills researchers have identified as critical for ATC include strong multitasking skills, an extraordinary level of sustained attention, and an excellent response speed (Eißfeldt & Heintz, 2002).

The contexts within which C2 operators perform are characterized by many factors that interact simultaneously (e.g., time pressure and information overload) and that are conducive to cognitive overload and potential detrimental cognitive effects (Hodgetts et al., 2014; Tremblay, Vachon, Lafond, & Kramer, 2012). Frequent management of complex and dynamic information by C2 operators, in addition to the concurrent management of these tasks, may lead to change blindness, the failure to detect even obvious changes in a visual scene (Durlach, 2004). Change blindness is known to occur when task demands reach or exceed one's attentional limits, thus explaining how certain changes in the environment can be ignored. Many situations, including those involving multitasking, can lead to attention overload and, ultimately, change blindness. In research demonstrating change blindness experienced by C2 controllers, Vachon, Vallières, Jones, and Tremblay (2012) assessed the eye gaze‐displacement patterns and pupil size (indicators of changes in attentional effort: Hoeks & Levelt, 1993) of participants performing a radar‐based risk assessment task in a simulated air warfare context involving a ship, and where they were required to perform three subtasks concurrently. The results indicated that changes in visual scenes were missed, either because they were not attended to or because of attentional failures resulting from cognitive overload experienced by participants as they attempted to attend to multiple sources of information simultaneously.

Interruptions are also characteristic of demanding C2 operator work environments, which are dynamic and often involve situations that require frequent updates to colleagues or superiors (Hodgetts, Tremblay, Vallières, & Vachon, 2015). Because the main tasks of C2 operators are frequently changing, situational awareness tends to be limited, especially if changes occur when the operator's attention is directed toward another task and is therefore less likely to be noticed (Smallman & St. John, 2003; St. John & Smallman, 2008). As the operators return to their main task, a resumption lag may be experienced, and efforts must be made to (a) identify any information that was potentially missed by updating situational awareness, (b) reactivate the goal of the main task, and (c) execute actions required to address the updated situation (Monsell, 2003). In summary, C2 operators may experience high cognitive load in completing not only their main tasks but by facing interruptions or task switching, which ultimately can lead to change blindness.

## 3.3 | Infantry and ground combat occupations

In combat operations, soldiers find themselves under persistent threats of attack and experience considerable anxiety (Nibbeling, Oudejans, Ubink, & Daanen, 2014). Situational awareness is crucial for infantry soldiers, especially those leading a team (Matthews, Strater, & Endsley, 2004). When a threat is detected, infantry soldiers must orient their attention to the new situation and may forget previously attended to information. Engaging in multiple tasks is often required such as detecting or recognizing signals in the environment, gathering field data, recalling mission‐related information, and planning and deciding to engage with a target (Cooke, Salas, Kiekel, & Bell, 2004). Because soldiers can be frequently interrupted by unforeseen events, understanding the cognitive limitations associated with their numerous tasks is critical. In particular, these limitations can lead to outcomes such as soldiers providing erroneous information to comrades, engaging in inappropriate actions, or incurring unnecessary risk.

According to Nibbeling et al. (2014), several basic cognitive skills are necessary to execute the duties of an infantry soldier including memory, vigilance, and making accurate decisions. By manipulating levels of stress, these authors showed that soldiers experienced increased anxiety and fatigue, which decreased their performance on many tasks, including those that required memory and vigilance. Although it is important for soldiers to combat fatigue and stress in order to be efficient at their job, they are frequently exposed to multitasking environments, which have been shown to increase fatigue (Chisholm, Collison, Nelson, & Cordell, 2000), heighten anxiety (Bailey & Konstan, 2006), and disrupt performance on vigilance tasks (Warm, Parasuraman, & Matthews, 2008).

According to Mitchell and Henthorn (2005), soldiers in surveillance operations are increasingly required to operate robots while also managing other tasks. In evaluating the effectiveness of new‐generation tanks, these authors found that tank gunners had fewer instances of cognitive overload than other tank occupants and were therefore the most viable candidates for performing robotics control tasks. However, they also discovered that gunners frequently dropped their primary task of target detection to execute more complex robotics tasks, which could be catastrophic if enemies were encountered. Controlling a robot can be difficult as it requires the operator to perform several simultaneous tasks (e.g., establishing the robot's position and issuing commands), which increases workload as the resources to execute the tasks exceed those actually available to complete them (Wickens & Tsang, 2015). Chen and Joyner (2006) showed that the ability of tank gunners to detect targets in their immediate environment is significantly affected if they are also required to monitor, manage, and remotely operate one or more robotic vehicles at the same time. Remote operation tasks induce cognitive overload, resulting in compromised performance on other tasks.

In summary, piloting, C2, and ground combat occupations often require soldiers to work on multiple tasks simultaneously. Indeed, these occupations involve the management of a large number of complex, sometimes simultaneous, tasks. For pilots, the challenge especially lies in switching from one demanding task to another and dividing attention between concurrent tasks. For C2 operators, the issue rather arises from the numerous task interruptions that can occur following strategic communication with colleagues, or because of the plethora of information they must attend to while performing their monitoring tasks. Finally, difficulties encountered by ground combat soldiers originate from the many tasks they must perform simultaneously or in alternation, while maintaining a high level of vigilance toward the environment to detect potential threats or targets. Despite their different origins, all these challenging contexts can ultimately lead to states of cognitive overload, compromising memory, and attention faculties, because tasks demands may exceed attentional resources available to manage them.

## 4 | SOLUTIONS FOR MORE EFFECTIVE MULTITASKING

In the previous section, we discussed how the main sources of errors stem from cognitive overload and memory deficits resulting from task interruptions, dual tasking, or task switching. Many studies propose effective solutions to prevent such errors arising from multitasking, either in military or civilian work environments. Given that these errors originate from limits in cognitive capacity, the following solutions aim to overcome those limits.

## 4.1 | Reducing cognitive overload

One way to overcome limits in cognitive capacity is to enable the user to decrease the task requirements or the structural limitations related to the task. These solutions aim to prevent the users from reaching or exceeding their available cognitive resources. For example, simple tools such as checklists and protocols can be used in the initial stages of a task to ensure that all procedures have been accomplished. This tends to lighten soldiers' workloads because their memory systems are not taxed by having to explicitly recall components of every task (Loukopoulos et al., 2009). Part‐task automation, that is, the usage of systems designed to carry out part of a task independently from the operator, can also be used to assist workers. These systems can support operators by completing some tasks automatically, which can reduce the users' workload and, in turn, increase their performance (Wickens, Hollands, Banbury, & Parasuraman, 2013). Many studies have shown that automation is a valuable asset for managing cognitive challenges and, when designed properly, may enhance human‐system performance in multitasking contexts (e.g., Harris, Hancock, Arthur, & Caird, 1995; Parasuraman, Sheridan, & Wickens, 2008, for reviews). Automated alerts can also be used to support operators' decisions. Alarms can be used to direct attention away from a highly‐demanding task to one that is more important, or to help operators recall forgotten information. Reminder cues can also be utilized to recall forgotten behavioral intentions after an interruption (Santangelo, 2015).

In some cases, the addition of alarms or automated tools can ironically add to cognitive load in an already overloaded perceptual environment (Vallières, Hodgetts, Vachon, & Tremblay, 2016). In order to decrease the cognitive load of individuals without using tools that may compromise performance, teamwork can be promoted, as the workload is shared between team members, and groups tend to have a greater processing capacity than single individuals for complex problem‐solving tasks (Kirschner, Paas, & Kirschner, 2008). Therefore, the use of teamwork could represent a simple solution that could help soldiers to perform simultaneous tasks more efficiently as it reduces the cognitive load for each individual.

Finally, certain tools are currently available to determine the most effective balance of soldier manpower and automation including CogTool (John, Prevas, Salvucci, & Koedinger, 2004) and the U.S. Army‐developed Improved Performance Research Integration Tool. Improved Performance Research Integration Tool, for example, is a dynamic simulation modeling tool that allows its users to evaluate interactions between human and automated performers, which can inform decisions for reallocation of tasks, where necessary (see Buck‐Gengler, Raymond, Healy, & Bourne Jr., 2012; Colombi, Miller, Schneidr, & McGrogan, 2012; Goodman, Miller, & Rusnock, 2015).

## 4.2 | Improving multitasking abilities

Even if studies identify solutions that can help to decrease cognitive load, these solutions may not be effective in every context. For instance, when the level of automation—that is, the extent to which the system is autonomic relative to the operator—is too high such as in a task switching context, switch cost times may be lengthened (Squire & Parasuraman, 2010). Performance in a multitasking context may also suffer if the system's reliability is suboptimal (Chen & Barnes, 2012). Moreover, even if teamwork helps reduce workload, many skills are still necessary for teams to be efficient, and numerous challenges may still be encountered particularly in complex and dynamic environments (NASA, 2014). Also, sometimes the nature of teamwork entails additional workload that would have been avoided by working individually (Nonose, Yoda, Kanno, & Furuta, 2015). Consequently, efforts must be made to improve soldiers' abilities to work in multitasking contexts where the tools' or group's efficiency or teamwork's reliability may decrease.

Soldiers' abilities to multitask can first be enhanced through training. Training in some occupations (e.g., aviation and C2) can help trainees develop their multitasking skills in the context of occupation‐specific tasks (Shadrick & Lussier, 2002). Walker et al. (2010) showed that, in a C2 context, military personnel with expertise demonstrated greater task accuracy and a deeper understanding in their decision‐making processes, compared with novices. According to Dux et al. (2009), training can improve multitasking performance through increasing processing speed of incoming information, allowing multiple tasks to be executed within the same time period. More particularly, training should emphasize such increase in processing speed, especially in contexts when operators must defer one task to shift their attention to another given that information about the deferred task can only be maintained in working memory for so long before being forgotten. In this case, retrieving the task set of the deferred task from memory would be more challenging, which might require unanticipated time and cognitive resources. Indeed, as Loukopoulos et al. (2009) point out, typically there are no alerts to prompt an operator to recall the deferred intention, and they must somehow “remember to remember.” Training can help to improve operator's recall accuracy and reaction time by providing specific strategies for encoding deferred behavioral intentions and creating effective retrieval cues. Another effective way to train multitasking abilities is through high‐fidelity simulations (e.g., microworlds) or using gaming platforms. Indeed, such platforms can capture the characteristics of multitasking environments (Hodgetts et al., 2015) and allow the user to learn appropriate behaviors transferrable to future training or real‐world situations (e.g., Kylesten & Nählinder, 2011).

Another method to ensure soldiers' enhanced multitasking abilities is to increase the selection of individuals with better multitasking abilities. Many studies show that individuals can differ greatly on their ability to perform while multitasking. Watson and Strayer (2010) have shown that some individuals—identified as “supertaskers”—can perform multiple tasks at the same time without compromising their performance on any particular task. The military should, therefore, implement selection tests aimed at trying to identify such individuals, as their performance would be less affected by the multitasking contexts in which they work, even if those environments involve more complex tasks. Attention to such individual differences in personnel selection tests should predict better on‐the‐job performance in multi-tasking‐related occupations, as such tests can match candidates to particular occupational profiles (Rimland & Larson, 1986). Morris, Daisley, Wheeler, and Boyer (2015) found that the cognitive skills assessed in personnel selection tests are valid predictors of job performance. Thus, military personnel selection tests should incorporate assessments of the candidates' multitasking abilities, by testing such abilities in realistic contexts that simulate their potential work environment. In fact, this type of assessment is already being used to select air traffic controllers and pilots, where candidates must complete selection tests that evaluate multitasking abilities (Pecena et al., 2013).

Although there are a number of existing multitasking assessments, research evaluating their usefulness in personnel screening and predictive validity to future job performance is limited (see Barron & Rose, 2017). The Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery is one such example of a personnel selection test currently used in the U.S. Military that has successfully predicted military training and job success (see Maier, 1993). Evidence shows that it can also predict multitasking performance (Hambrick et al., 2011), although many authors believe that the Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery lacks specificity and is in fact capturing general cognitive abilities (Jones & Ree, 1998). Indeed, many existing multitasking assessments capture multitasking ability more broadly (e.g., SynWin: Elsemore, 1994; dichotic listening test: Gopher & Kahneman, 1971). Therefore, more specific personnel selection tests should be created to assess particular forms of multitasking as they relate to specific military contexts, allowing for greater specificity on the relevant cognitive processes required to perform particular military tasks. Unfortunately, very few militaries incorporate even general multitasking assessments in their personnel selection process. Indeed, Mitchell and Driskell (1996) have suggested that studies using job analyses could permit to identify specific task requirements and the cognitive skills needed to perform them. Job analyses that identify critical multitasking skills could then guide human resource managers to improve the evaluation, selection, and appointment of military personnel to all occupations involving frequent multitasking work, including pilots, C2 operators, and infantry soldiers. This could improve performance in multitasking environments where accidents and hostilities are ever‐present risks, and mistakes can have major ramifications.

## 5 | OCCUPATION‐SPECIFIC SOLUTIONS FOR MORE EFFECTIVE MULTITASKING

As just mentioned, several solutions exist to ensure better performance in multitasking contexts by either reducing the cognitive load experienced by performing multiple tasks or by enhancing the multitasking abilities of the individuals who work in multitasking environments. In this section, we provide solutions that could be used in the specific military occupations previously discussed, according to the challenges and limitations unique in these contexts.

## 5.1 | Piloting

The overloaded work environments of pilots may lead them to occasionally forget to perform critical tasks, particularly when experiencing frequent task switching or interruption (Dismukes, 2007). Automated systems can help in this regard, such as the use of autopilot systems that can control some aspects of flight to decrease the pilot's workload (Sebok et al., 2012). When tasks cannot be automated, auditory alarms are a potential solution. Studies on alarm efficiency show that auditory alerts are useful even in complex and dynamic environments (Nardo, Santangelo, & Macaluso, 2011), with some auditory alerts currently being used in cockpits. Their usefulness comes from their ability to provide information on several urgent situations without requiring any change in head or eye movements (Edworthy, Loxley, & Dennis, 1991). However, alarms do have their limitations. Some aeronautic accidents have been associated with a lack of reaction to auditory alerts (Bliss, 2003). Alarms may be unintentionally ignored, especially when pilots exert excessive attentional focus induced by high‐workload situations such as the implementation of several tasks simultaneously (Dehais et al., 2014).

Another method that could help reorient pilots' attention in the cockpit toward relevant information is to use technological systems that remove, rather than add, stimuli that the worker is inappropriately focused on, and replace those stimuli with others designed to shift attentional focus toward more critical information. Such is the case with cognitive countermeasures, which are designed to prevent attentional tunneling, an inappropriate sustained focus of attention on noncrucial information and stressors. Dehais, Tessier, Christophe, and Reuzeau (2010) verified the impact of such systems in cases where pilots are focused on a task that requires considerable engagement and mental resources, to the point that they are unaware of their unstable runway approach. Because attentional tunneling makes attention shifting more difficult, the experimenters adjusted the pilots' interface to remove less relevant content and reveal more critical information. The results showed that this method lead pilots to overcome their task perseveration, detect the alert, and ultimately engage in operations required to stabilize the airplane. Cognitive countermeasures represent an effective alternative to alarms in reorienting attention toward a critical task when pilots are overwhelmed by the situation.

Another way to ensure that pilots can more efficiently manage simultaneous tasks is by recruiting individuals with enhanced multi-tasking abilities. For example, the procedure for selecting pilots at the German Aerospace Center (DLR) indirectly assesses multitasking abilities by testing cognitive skills such as memory and reasoning, and other academic and technical skills. According to Zierke (2014), this selection process test is efficient in selecting pilots who actually perform better. Thus, without including direct multitasking assessment, it seems that effective multitasking pilots can be recruited by using personnel selection tests that measure cognitive capacities associated with better multitasking abilities.

## 5.2 | Command and control

C2 soldiers often face stressful, complex, and dynamic situations in which they must perform several tasks in a short period of time. A frequent solution used by C2 operators to cope with these challenges is teamwork (Cooke et al., 2004). C2 operators face many interruptions, requiring both individuals and teams to coordinate and adapt quickly in order to maintain efficiency (LePine, 2005). In the context of a simulated firefighting situation, Tremblay et al. (2012) compared the effects of interrupting one or two members of a small team, relative to an individual operator, and found that interruptions were detrimental to operators' capacity to monitor resources, but that the harmful effects of interruption were reduced for those working in a team. This benefit of collaborative work seems to be explained or mediated by increased coordination and communication, with fewer post‐interruption communications and shorter interruption recoveries.

However, teamwork is not always an optimal solution because it requires supplementary actions that may induce cognitive overload (Nonose et al., 2015). Hence, automation and alarms are also used in C2 work environments such as the traffic alert and collision avoidance system, which triggers an alert when a potential aircraft collision is detected. This system is not always reliable and false alarms can occur, which can affect the performance of ATC operators (Dixon, Wickens, & McCarley, 2007). Even if alarms are perfectly reliable, certain features such as their saliency or their intensity may impact performance.

For example, Imbert et al. (2014) evaluated the impact of different types of visual alerts on their ability to capture attention by reporting participants' accuracy and speed in detecting critical events in a simulated ATC microworld setting. Results showed that more salient visual alarms captured attention more quickly, but alerts that are too intrusive can hinder performance on the primary task. This suggests that alert system designers should consider both an alert's capacity to reduce errors of omission (sufficient attentional power to reorient attention) and its attentional cost (low intrusiveness to prevent primary task disruption).

Change detection tools have been created for C2 operators to minimize the costs of alarms and help shield against change blindness caused by interruptions. These tools, accessed by the user's discretion, help to highlight changes in a situation that must be monitored by C2 operators (Durlach, 2004). For example, Smallman and St. John (2003) evaluated the efficiency of the Change History Explicit, a tool that promotes change awareness by logging significant changes to a situation in a radar‐based change‐detection task. Operators who used the tool were better at detecting aircraft changes and recovering situational awareness after an interruption, relative to those who did not use the tool. However, the efficiency of such change‐detection tools in multitasking contexts has recently been questioned. Vallières, Hodgetts, Vachon, and Tremblay (2012) showed that the Change History Explicit failed to improve change detection when embedded in a more realistic multitasking context involving threat evaluation and weapon assignment. Thus, the addition of tools such as alarms or logging tables is not always helpful, as they may exacerbate cognitive load.

Similar to pilots, C2 occupations can benefit from personnel selection tests that identify soldiers that will perform more effectively in multitasking contexts, such as the procedure for selecting ATC operators at the DLR. According to Pecena et al. (2013), the DLR procedure for selecting controllers includes measures of memory, processing speed, and attention, which have been found to be significantly correlated with several performance criteria of ATC training, a high multitasking‐based program.

## 5.3 | Infantry and ground combat occupations

The threats that infantry and ground combat occupation soldiers encounter, in addition to the main tasks they must perform for their mission, require multiple cognitive resources. Automation, like the use of unmanned vehicles and robots, can be used to execute tasks that would put the soldier at risk or tasks that would be difficult for soldiers to perform without placing them in a state of cognitive overload. Parasuraman, Cosenzo, and De Visser (2009) observed a supervision task of uninhabited vehicles and found that automation led to better change detection than manual control of the vehicles. Nevertheless, systems must be designed according to the context in which they are used. In particular, workload was reduced when the automation system was adaptive (on the basis of the individual operator's performance in real time) instead of model based (based on the prediction of general operator performance). When characteristics of the automated systems may lead to cognitive overload, cognitive countermeasures can be used. For example, using a real unmanned vehicle control task, Dehais, Causse, and Tremblay (2011)

induced an authority conflict between the operator and the robot known to promote attentional tunneling. During the conflict, half of the participants experienced a cognitive countermeasure wherein the part of the visual interface where excessive focus was recorded was temporarily removed and replaced by information related to new priorities, which led to more appropriate decision making and reductions in stress.

As stated earlier, technological systems must be sufficiently reliable in order to effectively support infantry soldiers. Chen and Barnes (2012) studied the impact of individual differences on the efficacy of a human‐automation system interaction in a multitasking situation using RoboLeader, a robot—in substitute of the human operator—that coordinates a team of lower capacity robots. This allows the human operator to deal with only the single entity (the robot) and focus their attention on other relevant tasks. Analyses showed that when the RoboLeader automation system was perfectly reliable, targets were detected faster than when the human operator directly managed and coordinated the robot team to locate targets in a remote simulated environment. However, when RoboLeader's reliability was less than optimal, that is, when the system provided inappropriate revisions of the robots' routes to locate the targets, participants' performance suffered. Still, individual differences such as attentional control capacity, video game experience, and spatial skills, moderated these effects and improved performance.

Infantry soldiers' multitasking abilities can also be trained. Although many studies address the effectiveness of training on individual task performance, fewer have evaluated the impact of training in multitasking contexts. This is particularly true in the case of ground combat occupations where most studies deal primarily with soldiers' technical skills, with less of a focus on cognitive skills, except general intelligence (Jones & Ree, 1998). Some studies have assessed the impacts of training on tasks that are indirectly related to multitasking such as evaluating the efficacy of vehicle crew trainings and driving capacities. Goode, Salmon, and Lenné (2013) conducted a review of vehicle simulations used to train civilian or military drivers and vehicle crew, and whether simulations are effective at enhancing drivingrelated procedural and higher order cognitive skills, as well as team‐related procedural and nontechnical skills. According to their review, trainees can learn procedural and higher order cognitive skills that can be transferred to real‐world driving behaviors, although some studies on procedural skills suffer from validity issues. However, specific high‐order cognitive skills such as visual processing, attentional deployment, and reaction time seem to be effectively improved by these simulations, which are generalizable to real‐life driving situations. Because driving is an activity that entails the management of multiple simultaneous subtasks (Courage, Bakhtiar, Fitzpatrick, Kenny, & Brandeau, 2015), it seems that militaries can in fact become more proficient on such multitasking activity by training using realistic simulations.

In this section, we have outlined strategies that can be used by pilots, C2 operators, and infantry soldiers working in multitasking contexts that can mitigate or prevent potential negative effects of multitasking on performance. Many solutions have already been implemented in these military domains. However, the tools and procedures used to multitask more efficiently should be implemented in consideration of the nature of the applied context. Moreover, methods to reduce cognitive overload and improve soldiers' multitasking abilities can complement one another. For instance, if a tool's reliability was jeopardized, a highly‐trained soldier would likely react more effectively than a soldier with minimal training in multitasking contexts.

## 6 | CONCLUSIONS

Multitasking is cognitively challenging for operators placed in complex and dynamic work situations such as the military. Regarding military occupations in particular, compromises in military personnel's cognitive capacities could have critical and lethal consequences, potentially jeopardizing the security of civilians, military personnel, critical equipment, and infrastructure. Knowledge of the cognitive limitations arising from multitasking contexts is crucial and must be integrated into military programs to ensure that people in uniform have the resources to be effective in their missions. This knowledge would also benefit military commanders who could apply the solutions described here to reduce the workload in occupations characterized by the management of several tasks or to improve the cognitive capacities of the soldiers to help them better manage their current tasks.

One of the main solutions that can mitigate the harmful impacts of multitasking is the use of technological devices that remind one of forgotten tasks or decrease soldiers' cognitive loads. Indeed, the existing literature indicates that certain technologies can be beneficial for workers in multitasking contexts, helping them manage the high workload associated with the performance of many complex dynamic tasks. However, as discussed in this review, even if such systems can reduce workload by helping one manage multiple tasks or by directing one's focus to a more critical task, they can sometimes compromise performance, particularly when the level of automaticity is too high (Squire & Parasuraman, 2010), when the tools' reliability is suboptimal (Chen & Barnes, 2012), when their intrusiveness is too high (Imbert et al., 2014), or when they are simply not appropriate for a multitasking context (Vallières et al., 2012). Cautious design of support tools is key, with features adapted to the multitasking context. As reiterated by Lafond, Vachon, Rousseau, and Tremblay (2010), the effectiveness of systems should be assessed, taking into account global effects on cognitive functioning and performance, not simply targeted cognitive functioning. Specifically, one needs to consider whether the benefits of addressing one aspect of the situation do not happen at the expense of other important aspects (see also Vachon, Lafond, Vallières, Rousseau, & Tremblay, 2011). The adoption of a holistic approach in the designing and evaluation of aid systems should help maximize the cognitive benefits such systems provide in multitasking situations, while minimizing their potential drawbacks.

Hiring individuals with better multitasking abilities and training soldiers appropriately can ensure that they will be more efficient at managing tasks simultaneously. Unfortunately, few military domains currently incorporate multitasking assessment in their personnel selection process. Further, although multitasking training is not common in all military domains, some platforms currently exist that allow training to be adapted to the relevant context (Hodgetts et al., 2015; Kylesten & Nählinder, 2011). Mitchell and Driskell (1996) have suggested that studies using job analyses could identify specific task requirements and cognitive skills needed to perform them. Job analyses that identify crucial multitasking skills could then guide human resource managers in evaluating, selecting, and appointing personnel to positions. In addition, such knowledge could help human resource managers to develop and/or adapt military performance tests for positions where multitasking is a central component of the job, and design training procedures appropriate for the multitasking context within which soldiers must operate.

In conclusion, multitasking is highly prevalent in several military occupations and tends to lead to performance deficits and disruptions of cognitive functioning that should not be overlooked. Solutions have been identified to address these issues, but their effectiveness may be limited by the demands inherent in such multitasking contexts. This means that such solutions could apply to other military or nonmilitary occupations where individuals must juggle multiple tasks or defer an intended action (e.g., first responders, air traffic controllers, and closed‐circuit television operators), as long as the characteristics of the working context are taken into account. Multitasking should be a chief concern for both operational planning and training development, as the amount and complexity of information to be processed by soldiers will not decrease in years to come. In implementing relevant solutions discussed here, soldiers would be better equipped to manage multiple tasks simultaneously, helping to prevent critical errors that could compromise the security of military personnel and civilians.
