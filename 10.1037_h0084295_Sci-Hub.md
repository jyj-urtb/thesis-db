# Canadian Journal of Psychology Outstanding Contributions Series

Dual Coding Theory: Retrospect and Current Status

Allan Paivio  
University of Western Ontario

ABSTRACT This review traces the highlights of almost 30 years of research on imagery and verbal processes and the progressive translation of that empirical base into the dual coding theory (DCT) of memory and cognition. General and specific criticisms of the theory and research findings are also addressed, focussing especially on alternative views that emphasize abstract propositional representations as the basis of cognition. The first part of the review deals with the origins of DCT in research related to the conceptual peg hypothesis of concreteness and imagery effects on associative memory and with the subsequent unpacking and expansion of the explicit and implicit assumptions embodied in that hypothesis. These assumptions, of which DCT is comprised, pertain to the structural and functional properties of nonverbal and verbal representational systems and their empirical implications for memory, language, and cognition generally. The final section brings the story up to date with a review of empirical and conceptual responses to some recent criticisms of DCT and alternative theoretical views in areas related to concreteness effects on memory, implicit memory, schema theory, and conceptual issues in philosophy of science.

Des critiques générales et spécifiques de la théorie et des résultats expérimentaux y sont aussi abordées avec une emphase particulière sur des propositions alternatives qui appuient les représentations propositionnelles abstraites comme la base de la cognition. La première partie de la revue traite des origines de la DCT dans la recherche reliée à l'hypothèse conceptuelle de la concrétude et les effets de l'imagerie sur la mémoire associative ainsi que les refontes et l'expansion des assomptions implicites et explicites comprises dans cette hypothèse. Dual coding theory (DCT) evolved from specific experiments on the role of imagery in associative learning (Paivio, 1963, 1965). I recognized then that the approach and results departed from the verbal learning and memory tradition of that period, but could not foresee how quickly the research would expand and lead to a general theory of memory and cognition. The systematic empirical and theoretical programme was first described in an address to the Canadian Psychological Association (Paivio, 1967a) and then presented more comprehensively in reviews (Paivio, 1969, 1971) that attracted scientific attention (Garfield, 1978a, 1978b) and apparently helped motivate imagery research by others. In any case, the subsequent research developments and theoretical debates involving imagery were generally quite remarkable and at times dramatic (e.g., see Paivio, 1986).

Why was the early DCT research noticed so promptly? The most important reason might be that it was the first systematic objective approach to the study of imagery and its functions. The concept of imagery has always played a central role in speculations about the nature of memory and thought, and it became the principal object of introspective study in the structuralist research programme before and after the turn of the century. A parallel approach, initiated by Galton, focussed on individual differences in reported vividness of imagery. These subjective approaches proved to be unfruitful and vulnerable to Watson's behaviouristic attack on the introspective method and on consciousness as the object of study. Thus, imagery phenomena and methodology became anathema in mainstream North American psychology. The occasional imagery studies that appeared here and elsewhere were sporadic and unsystematic, with a continued emphasis on subjective vividness as the principal defining attribute of imagery.

The dual coding approach took a different turn from the outset. Imagery was objectively measured by procedures that could be systematically related to performance in memory and other tasks. These independent imagery variables included (a) the image-evoking value of words and larger language structures, (b) experimental procedures designed to encourage or interfere with the use of imagery, and (c) individual differences in imagery abilities. These constituted a set of convergent operations for measuring and manipulating imagery. Subjective reports of imagery strategies and experiences supplemented those procedures but were never relied on as the sole indicators of imagery involvement. A parallel set of defining procedures converged on verbal processes to complete the dual coding operational framework. This approach turned out to be generally successful as an organizational and heuristic research base for developing and testing the hypotheses and assumptions that characterized DCT as it expanded.

The convergent-operational approach and the theory have also encountered obstacles along the way. Early in the programme, individual difference measures of imagery proved to be inconsistent predictors of performance in tasks that were presumed to be imagery mediated (Paivio, 1971, chap. 14). The theory itself has been the object of specific criticisms and has been implicated in continuing controversies concerning the status of imagery in human cognition and the nature of cognitive representations in general. Whereas DCT rests on the assumption that the representational substrate is multimodal and relatively concrete, the dominant opposing position has been that the language of thought is unimodal and abstract, classically viewed as internalized words and sentences and, more recently, as amodal propositional structures and rule-governed (computational) processes.

The purpose of the present review is to evaluate DCT against the alternatives, beginning with the origin and development of DCT from 1963 to the date of the last comprehensive review (Paivio, 1986) and ending with an appraisal of the current status of DCT. A reconsideration of the early history is justified because most of the current issues have their roots in the past and because the critics of DCT have never addressed the full range of evidence that supports the various assumptions and hypotheses that make up the theory. I hope to show that, when all of the pertinent facts are considered, DCT remains solid over most of the empirical domain it encompasses. But there are important exceptions as well, and I address these in the final section of the review.

## OVERVIEW OF DUAL CODING THEORY

This summary of the current form of DCT provides an orienting framework for the retrospective review of events that led up to the theory. The theory retains the basic assumptions of the earlier version (Paivio, 1971), but the present treatment is more systematic and includes modifications that were prompted by new data and theoretical issues that emerged since 1971. The assumptions are listed in Table 1. I comment on those that are especially relevant to the subsequent research review.

## Distinction Between Symbolic and Sensorimotor Systems

The theory assumes an orthogonal relation between symbolic systems and specific sensorimotor systems. Verbal and nonverbal systems symbolically represent the structural and functional properties of language and the nonlinguistic world, respectively. However, both classes of events come in different modalities — visual (printed words vs. visual objects), auditory (spoken words vs. environmental sounds), haptic (tactual and motor feedback from writing vs. manipulation of objects) — and the internal symbolic systems presumably retain these distinctions. The orthogonal pattern is incomplete because gustatory, olfactory, and affective modalities are nonverbal by definition, in that we do not construct linguistic symbols from tastes, smells, or emotional experiences. The orthogonal model is fully justified by empirical data, including neuropsychological deficits associated with focal lesions in different parts of the brain (see below).

This assumption makes it plain that DCT is a multiple coding theory, with a special emphasis on the fundamental importance of the verbal/nonverbal symbolic contrast. The multiple sensorimotor side of the theory has often been overlooked, perhaps because DCT research has emphasized visual imagery more than other modalities, just as other imagery researchers have done. The literature also includes more specific misinterpretations in which dual coding has been characterized as a distinction between visual and verbal systems (which incorrectly contrasts a specific sensory modality with a general symbolic modality) or between images and propositional representations (the latter have never been a part of DCT). My disclaimer concerning such interpretations applies to early as well as recent versions of DCT. For example, many sections in Imagery and Verbal Processes (Paivio, 1971) refer explicitly to imagery modalities other than visual (e.g., pp. 518-520), the necessity of postulating multiple modalities of verbal representations (pp. 55-56), and the general contrast between symbolic and sensory processing systems (pp. 149-151, 239-240). Moreover, the sensory/symbolic orthogonal model was explicitly laid out just 1 year later (Paivio, 1972). The conceptual oversights and misinterpretations by others have not had any important empirical consequences as far as I can tell, but this should set the record straight in any case.

## Representational Units

The representational units of the two systems are assumed to be modality-specific perceptual-motor analogues. I referred to these originally as verbal representations and imaginal representations (Paivio, 1971), but later (Paivio, 1978) I adopted the terms logogen (word generator), borrowed from Morton (1969), and imagen (image generator) to distinguish the underlying structural representations from their expression as consciously experienced images and inner speech. Modality specificity means that different representations correspond to the visual, auditory, and other sensorimotor modalities of the represented external entities and response patterns. The units are assumed to vary in size and complexity while still functioning as integrated informational structures or response generators for some purposes: More complex units are constructed from smaller ones, hierarchically (a face consists of eyes, nose, lips; eyes of iris and pupils; etc.) or linearly (a word is a concatenation of sounds). This is a componential approach in which the components are concrete, modality-specific, holistic analogues rather than abstract, amodal features.

## System-Level Properties

A series of assumptions refer to the structural and functional properties of the symbolic systems as a whole.

Functional Independence of Subsystems: Verbal and nonverbal systems are assumed to be functionally independent in that one system can be active without the other or both can be active in parallel. One important implication of this assumption is that verbal and nonverbal codes corresponding to the same object (e.g., pictures and their names) can have additive effects on recall. The independence assumption extends to sensorimotor systems as well, with parallel implications (e.g., additive memory effects of visual and auditory aspects of such objects as telephones, whistles, and bells).

Interunit Connections: Different representational units must be functionally interconnected so that activity can spread from one unit to others, between and within systems. Referential interconnections link imagens and logogens, permitting objects to be named and names to evoke images. The interconnections are one-to-many, in both directions (an object can have many names and a name, many different referents), and activation is probabilistically determined by the strength of different interconnections interacting with the stimulus context. Associative interconnections link units within systems, so that activity can spread among logogens (as in word associations) or imagens (one object reminds us of another), again in a probabilistic fashion.

Processing Operations: Like other cognitive theories, DCT includes mechanisms for accessing and activating mental representations, directly or indirectly, using the structural interconnections in the fashion just described. Thus, activation is a probabilistic function of stimulus variables (e.g., word concreteness, meaningfulness, familiarity), contextual stimuli (e.g., task instructions), and individual difference variables (e.g., imagery or verbal ability).

The theory distinguishes three different levels or kinds of processing: Representational processing refers to the relatively direct activation of logogens by linguistic stimuli and imagens by nonverbal stimuli; referential processing refers to the cross-system activation required in imaging to words and naming objects; and associative processing entails activation of representations within either system, accounting for spread of association among words or among images. All cognitive tasks require representational processing and some may involve all three. For example, the word dog activates the corresponding logogen and, under appropriate contextual conditions, could also activate the verbal associate cat and eventuate in an image of a cat and dog.

Organizational and Transformational Processes: The reference here is to active organization of incoming verbal or nonverbal information and subsequent mental transformation of the resulting structures. The main theoretical distinction is that verbal and nonverbal processes organize and transform information differently, in accordance with the respective structural constraints of the two systems. The verbal system generates sequential structures of different levels of complexity (e.g., phrases, sentences) and transformations are constrained by that sequential frame, so that the temporal order of elements can be changed or new elements substituted for ones that occupy a particular temporal slot. Nonverbal transformations are governed by the structural and processing constraints associated with nonverbal representations. Thus, they include transformations on any spatial dimension (e.g., mental rotations, changes in the size or shape of images, etc.), changes in sensory properties (imagined colour, sound quality, etc.), movement of imaginal objects, and so on.

Other assumptions pertain to (a) the role of consciousness in DCT and (b) basic adaptive functions served by the representational systems, which include evaluative, mnemonic, and motivational/emotional functions. These aspects will be discussed in relevant research contexts, with mnemonic functions getting most of the attention throughout. I begin with the events that prompted and shaped the various assumptions in the course of the development of DCT.

## DEVELOPMENT OF DUAL CODING THEORY

The research that led to DCT was motivated by the verbal climate that dominated the psychology of human learning, memory, and thought. The objects of study were nonsense syllables and words whose meaningfulness was defined by verbal associations (e.g., Noble, 1952), and the thought processes that augmented rote learning were interpreted as verbal mediators, which also were inferred from associative data or induced by various language-based experimental procedures.

The DCT programme systematically reshaped that framework by extending the definition of meaning to include the image-evoking capacity of stimuli, inducing imaginal mediators experimentally, and inferring imagery use from postexperimental questionnaires. These imagery variables were compared with parallel verbal ones in controlled studies designed to reveal their separate and joint contributions to learning and memory. The enterprise was guided by hypotheses and assumptions that initiated and were generated by the research and contributed progressively to the general theory. These conceptual developments are reviewed chronologically below, with anticipatory hints and flashbacks to show the emerging theoretical coherence of the ideas.

## The Conceptual-Peg Hypothesis

The kernel from which DCT evolved is the conceptual-peg hypothesis of word imagery effects on recall, which implicates processes that became basic assumptions in the general theory. The hypothesis originated as a metaphorical extension of the “one-bun, two-shoc” rhyming mnemonic technique, which I had learned before

I became a student of psychology. Ancient and modern mnemonists have used this technique or variants of it to remember long lists of items with apparent ease, and all memory researchers today are familiar with it.

The mnemonic technique explicitly requires dual coding in that nonverbal images are initially generated from words during list learning, then regenerated from verbal cues during recall, and finally decoded back into words. For example, lamp as the first item to be remembered could be associated with the peg word bun by imaging a lamp as the filler inside a hamburger bun. The words one-bun subsequently prompt recall of the compound image from which the response component, lamp, can be retrieved. The technique implicates at least the following DCT processes: (a) verbal/imaginal referential processing, (b) verbal associative processing (based partly on rhyming relations), and (c) imagery organization (integration). Together, the verbal and imaginal components contribute to distinctive encoding and efficient retrieval of information from memory.

The conceptual-peg hypothesis extended the mnemonic technique's rationale to associative memory tasks in general. In the technique, the memory cues (e.g., one-bun) are generated by the memorizer using the rhyming relation, but stimulus words can serve the same mnemonic function when they are explicitly presented during learning. The most salient characteristic of memory peg words is their concreteness and strong capacity to evoke images. A critical implication of the analysis for the paired-associates task is that concreteness should facilitate recall more as a property of stimulus words than of responses. Accordingly, assuming that nouns are generally more concrete than adjectives, Lambert and Paivio (1956) predicted and confirmed that recall would be better when nouns serve as stimuli for adjectives than vice versa. I confirmed the hypothesis more directly in subsequent experiments by varying the concreteness and image-evoking value of the nouns in noun-adjective pairs (Paivio, 1963) and noun-noun pairs (Paivio, 1965). The latter study included concrete (C) nouns and abstract (A) nouns in all possible stimulus-response combinations. The critical result was that cued recall of pair types increased in the order A-A, A-C, C-A, and C-C, showing that concreteness had a strong positive effect that was greater on the stimulus than on the response side of pairs. Independent rating data established, moreover, that the concrete words evoked images much more easily than the abstract words.

## Empirical and Conceptual Issues

The conceptual-peg experiments raised a series of issues concerning (a) the locus of the concreteness effect, (b) whether imagery really is the effective variable, (c) the structural properties of effective mediating images, and (d) the role of verbal processes in the effects.

Processing Locus of the Concreteness Effect: The conceptual-pcg hypothesis implies that the greater effect of concreteness on the stimulus side of pairs is a retrieval, rather than an encoding, phenomenon. Whereas the concreteness of both members determines how easy it is to construct a compound image during encoding, the concreteness of the retrieval cue determines how easily that image can be redintegrated during recall. This retrieval interpretation was confirmed by experiments in which either the nominal stimulus or the nominal response word (the left- or right-hand member) of each studied pair was presented as the cue on the recall trial: Recall depended on the concreteness of the retrieval cue rather than on its position during the study trial (e.g., Yarmey & O'Neill, 1969; for a summary, see Paivio, 1971, pp. 281-283).

The hypothesis also assumes that the images evoked by concrete stimuli function as associative mediators, but another possibility is that concreteness has its effect by enhancing stimulus distinctiveness, so that pair encodings are stable and easily differentiated from each other. I tested and tentatively rejected the latter interpretation in the 1965 experiment using verbal associative overlap as a measure of item differentiation: Associative overlap scores did not differ for the abstract and concrete words used in that experiment. However, subsequent studies by others led to the modified conclusion that concrete stimuli (words or pictures) promote generation of images that are both distinct and associatively effective (Paivio, 1971, pp. 293-294). The two functions of imagery were experimentally dissociated by Rowe and Paivio (1971), who showed that imagery aided verbal discrimination learning (identifying the experimenter-defined “correct” word in each of a series of pairs) when subjects imaged only to the correct word in each pair rather than to both words, whereas incidental cued recall following discrimination learning was aided by compound images to both pair members and not by single-word images. This finding is one of the most telling rejoinders to a recent criticism of DCT (see below).

Is Imagery the Variable That Accounts for Concreteness Effects? The relations between stimulus concreteness, imagery value, and memory are correlational. Thus, the causal relation was indeterminate because other word attributes also correlate with concreteness and any of these could be related to word memorability. For reasons already discussed, verbal associative meaningfulness (m) was the most prominent alternative to imagery in the 1960s. A series of experiments showed that the relation between stimulus imagery value and recall was undiminished when m was held constant, whereas m had little effect when imagery was controlled (Paivio, Smythe, & Yuille, 1968). A factor analytic study (Paivio, 1968) similarly ruled out more than 20 other word attributes as possible predictive alternatives to imagery and concreteness. Rated case of imagery was, in fact, the best single predictor of recall scores for words, especially when the words served as stimulus members in paired associated learning (as compared to response members or items in free recall lists). Richardson (1980) subsequently obtained some evidence that concreteness might be independently effective, but further results led him to conclude that imagery value probably is the effective underlying variable. Doubts continue to be expressed, however, and so this issue, too, must be re-addressed in a later section.

The increasing evidence that rated imagery value was the best single predictor of word recall did not necessarily mean that the effects were actually mediated by imagery processes. Accordingly, the imagery interpretation was tested experimentally using additional manipulations and indicators of imagery. One involved comparisons of object pictures and their names as items on the assumption that pictures arouse memory images even more directly than concrete words. Consistent with that view, paired-associates studies showed that recall was higher with pictures than with words as stimulus members, but not necessarily when pictures were the to-be-recalled response members of pairs (e.g., Dilley & Paivio, 1968; Paivio & Yarmey, 1966).

Postexperimental strategy questionnaires provided further relevant evidence. The reported frequency of imagery use during paired-associate learning of high imagery and low imagery nouns in the Paivio, Smythe, and Yuille (1968) experiment correlated substantially with learning when the items served as stimuli  $r = .56$  and less strongly when they were responses  $r = .39$ , thereby reinforcing the conceptual peg view that learning is especially dependent on imagery evoked by the words used as retrieval cues. Other interpretations were also considered and rejected on logical and empirical grounds. For example, subjects often reported using verbal strategies as well, but the verbal reports did not correlate significantly with recall. This observation is inconsistent with any verbal/mediational interpretation of concreteness and imagery effects on recall.

The results just described were based on item analyses using average strategy and learning scores for pairs. As further confirmation of the imagery interpretation, Richardson (1978) found that individual differences in reported use of imagery correlated more highly than use of verbal strategies with the subjects' recall scores for concrete pairs but not abstract pairs.

Experimental manipulation of learning strategies coupled with strategy reports and pairs varying in concreteness provided the most definitive convergent evidence for the imagery interpretation. Paivio and Yuille (1969) gave different groups of subjects instructions to use imagery, verbal mediators, or rote memorization to learn noun pairs. Within each condition, different subgroups were questioned about their learning strategies following one, two, or three study-test trials. Those questioned after one trial generally reported following their assigned strategy. Those questioned after the second or third trial shifted progressively away from apparently inappropriate or inefficient instructional strategies and toward the pattern predicted from DCT (e.g., from imagery to verbal mediators or rote repetition with abstract pairs). Moreover, reported imagery use correlated highly with recall scores for concrete but not abstract pairs. Finally, other experiments showed that (a) following imagery learning instructions, recall of the original mediating image (as indicated by verbal reports) was associated with correct response recall (Yuille, 1973), and (b) verbally reported recall of the image can precede recall of the target response (May & Clayton, 1973). These results justify a causal interpretation of the role of imagery in associative learning.

The Structure of Effective Mediating Images: In the context of the conceptual-peg hypothesis, the associative mediators were described as compound images that could be reinstated by presentation of a retrieval cue. Such compounds were later characterized as interactive or integrative images. Epstein, Rock, and Zuckerman (1960; see also Wollen, 1969) had shown that picture pairs are learned more easily when the pair members are pictured as interactive units (e.g., a hand inside a bowl) than when they are shown as separate units (e.g., a hand beside a bowl). Bower (1970) obtained similar results using imagery instructions: Cued recall was higher when his participants had been asked to form interactive images of the referents of word pairs than after separate imagery instructions. Begg (1972) operationalized the effective imagery variable even more precisely: Using concrete phrases (e.g., white horse) and abstract phrases (e.g., basic truth), he found that cuing by one member of the pair increased recall relative to a free recall condition only in the case of the concrete phrases. Begg concluded that the differential cuing effect can be interpreted in terms of integrated imaginal memory traces that are redintegrated by high imagery words. The free-to-cued-recall increment, introduced by Horowitz and Prytulak (1969), became the standard operational indicator of integrative imagery and other integrative processes. It has helped resolve a persistent DCT issue to which I return later on.

Verbal Processes in Early DCT Research: Although verbal processes were insufficient to account for the effects of imagery variables in associative learning, this does not mean that they were ineffective. Obviously, verbal mechanisms are directly implicated whenever words are used as stimuli or when verbal responses are required in memory tasks. This verbal contribution was taken for granted in the early DCT research, and imagery was viewed as a supplementary code added to the verbal baseline. An important goal of the early research was to distinguish the memory contributions of the two codes. In the first experiment of that kind, Paivio and Yuille (1967) found that verbal mediation instructions and imagery instructions both increased associative learning relative to rote repetition instructions. The interpretation of those results was unclear, however, in that subjects in the verbal condition could also have imaged during the task and, similarly, those in the imagery condition could have elaborated verbally while attempting to generate images. From the dual coding perspective, it was expected that verbal instructions would be more effective than imagery instructions with abstract pairs because verbal associates can be generated more easily than images to such pairs (Yuille & Paivio, 1967). The reverse was expected for concrete pairs. The predicted interaction did not occur in the Paivio and Yuille (1967) experiment, but it was finally obtained by Paivio and Foth (1970) using stringent imagery and verbal mediation criteria: Recall was higher following imagery than verbal instructions with concrete pairs and vice versa with abstract pairs. The effect was attributed to relatively more failures to generate images in the abstract case rather than to ineffectiveness of imaginal mediators once generated.

Other procedures have also been used to investigate the relative contributions of verbal and imaginal processes in associative learning (see Paivio, 1971, chap. 11), but the above examples suffice to illustrate the problem and how researchers tried to solve it. We turn next to other memory tasks and direct tests of the dual coding independence assumption.

## Independence and Additivity of Codes

The evidence that imagery benefits associative learning when relevant verbal factors are controlled implies that the two processing systems can function independently. Moreover, the idea that imagery supplements a verbal processing baseline suggests that image and verbal codes have additive effects. The additivity hypothesis was first proposed explicitly in the DCT programme to explain the superiority of concrete over abstract words in free recall (Paivio, 1967b) and then extended to the picture superiority effect (Paivio, Rogers, & Smythe, 1968). The theoretical rationale was that the probability of dual coding (and recall) decreases from pictures to concrete words to abstract words because subjects in memory experiments are highly likely to name pictures of familiar objects covertly during learning, somewhat less likely to image to concrete nouns, and least likely to image to abstract nouns (Paivio, 1971, pp. 179-180). A further possibility was that the recall effects were due entirely to mnemonic superiority of the image code over the verbal code.

Paivio and Csapo (1973) tested alternative hypotheses by inducing participants to name or generate images to pictures or words and then unexpectedly asking them to recall the words or the names of the pictures. The result was that imaging to words doubled recall relative to words that had only been pronounced; moreover, the imaged words were recalled about as well as named pictures. These and other results supported the code-additivity hypothesis and suggested as well that the contribution of the image (or pictorial) code was greater than that of the verbal code, perhaps by a 2:1 ratio in these experiments.

The strongest tests of the additivity hypothesis were done using the repetition-lag paradigm of free recall (Paivio, 1975a). The lag effect refers to the fact that recall of repeated items in a list increases with the numbers of other items that intervene between the repeated events. This increase often asymptotes at a level that would be expected if the repeated items are functionally independent and, therefore, additive in their effect. Recall of successively repeated (0-lag) items, however, is usually less than additive but can be increased by contextual factors or other procedures designed to encourage differential (semantic) encoding of the item on different occasions (e.g., Madigan, 1969). The lag effect entails puzzles that are interesting in their own right, but the relevant point here is that recall of successively repeated items can be increased by differential encoding.

The DCT assumption that imaginal and verbal codes are mnemonically independent and additive led to the prediction that conceptual repetition induced by a picture and its name would produce an additive recall effect even under the 0-lag condition. This was tested using lists that included repeated pictures (PP), repeated words (WW), and picture-word (PW or WP) repetitions. Recall levels for unrepeated pictures (P) and words (W) in the list provided the baseline for estimating the recall probabilities that would be expected if the repeated events were mnemonically independent. The results were as predicted: PP and WW were less than additive at 0-lag and became additive only at long lags, whereas PW recall was additive even at 0-lag. In addition, P recall exceeded W recall in the case of repeated items as well as unrepeated items, reinforcing the conclusion that the mnemonic value of pictures is greater than that of words.

The same procedure was then used to study the effects of repeated imaginal and verbal coding. Thus, using only words as stimuli, subjects were prompted by coding cues to image to a repeated word on each occurrence (II), or code it verbally by pronouncing it covertly each time (VV), or verbally on one occasion and imaginally on the other (VI). Unrepeated items also were coded in one way or the other. The crucial results were that VI dual coding had an additive effect even at 0-lag, whereas II and VV were less than additive. Moreover, I coding produced higher recall than

V coding, so that the overall pattern was remarkably similar to the pattern obtained using picture and word repetitions (Paivio, 1975a, Fig. 1).

The conclusions from the repetition-lag experiments were that (a) verbal and non-verbal memory codes are statistically independent and additive in their effect on free recall, (b) the nonverbal code is mnemonically stronger than the verbal code, and (c) the relative effects of the two codes are the same whether induced perceptually (P and W presentation) or generated from long-term memory by coding cues (I and V). Dual coding theory was originally silent on possible quantitative differences in memorability of verbal and nonverbal codes, but image superiority can now be incorporated into the theory as an empirical generalization, comparable to experimentally determined quantitative values for theoretical functions in other sciences. These observations and the general independence assumption of DCT remain relevant throughout the remainder of this article, but it is appropriate at this point to confront some specific criticisms and alternative interpretations.

Criticisms and Further Evidence Concerning Code Additivity: One type of criticism is that the recall effects reviewed above could be explained by levels of processing or elaborative processing approaches without recourse to the verbal/nonverbal coding distinction. J.R. Anderson (1978), for example, stated that reliance on picture/word differences was fading because pictures were not necessarily better remembered if words were “deeply encoded.” Anderson’s empirical generalization is correct even in the present context, for we found that the usual picture-superiority effect was eliminated when words were subjected to (deep) imagery encoding. The question is whether the difference also disappears when there is no elaborative coding of either pictures or words or when words are processed elaboratively without using imagery.

The repetition experiments (Paivio, 1975a) involved incidental as well as intentional learning conditions, and recall of pictures exceeded words (P > W and PP > WW) under all conditions. Thus, picture superiority emerged when elaborative processing was unlikely. What about the effect of nonimaginal elaborative processing? Paivio and Lambert (1981, Exp. 1) required bilingual subjects to write down the English names of pictures and translate concrete French nouns into English during their presentation. An unexpected free recall test showed that recall was twice as high for picture-generated words than for translated words. Note that there is no a priori reason to suppose that naming pictures is any deeper or more elaborate than translating words. Thus, the results favour a dual coding interpretation in which the nonverbal trace component (picture plus generated English word) contributes more to the additive effect than does a verbal component (French word plus its generated English translation). This conclusion was strengthened by the finding that translated words were recalled twice as well as English words that were simply copied, indicating that the mnemonic superiority of picture naming was not due to attenuated processing in the translation condition. (The translation effect is relevant to a bilingual extension of DCT, to be described later.)

Dual coding was directly contrasted with the depth approach by D'Agostino, O'Neill, and Paivio (1977) using pictures, concrete words, and abstract words as items, along with encoding operations that reflected increasingly deep (or elaborate) processing according to Craik and Tulving (1975). The levels approach implies that recall should increase with depth for all three types of items, whereas DCT does not predict a uniform increase. The relevant prediction here is that phonemic and deeper semantic processing of pictures will result in equivalent recall of pictures because both conditions require the pictures to be named before the phonemic or semantic decisions can be made. The results were as predicted: phonemic and semantic conditions produced equivalent recall levels, both exceeding a (“shallow”) visual/structural condition that did not require naming.

The above studies demonstrate that picture superiority in free recall cannot be explained easily in terms of processing levels or elaboration without also considering the structural differences between pictorial and verbal codes. The results are readily explained, however, by the code-additivity assumption of DCT, augmented by non-verbal code superiority.

The case for DCT can be made just as readily using instructionally induced imaginal and verbal coding tasks. The most telling observation, already mentioned above, was that imaging doubles free recall relative to a verbal coding (pronunciation) condition. The processing depth counterargument would be that imaging ensures deeper (more elaborate) semantic processing than does pronunciation. This was refuted by a further bilingual memory experiment by Paivio and Lambert (1981, Exp. 2). Bilinguals were presented lists of English words along with coding cues that induced them to image to one third of the words, translate one third into French, and copy one third. They were then asked unexpectedly to recall the English stimulus words they had been shown. Recall doubled at each step from copy, to translation, to image conditions, closely paralleling the pattern obtained when subjects recalled English names generated to English words, French words, and pictures. The increase from translation to image conditions specifically supports independent (additive) contributions of a verbal code and a mnemonically superior imaginal code. The case may not be closed yet, but I am unaware of any telling refutations to date of the DCT interpretation of the results just described.

The DCT interpretation of picture superiority in free recall has also been questioned in the case of recognition memory. Recognition memory scores are generally higher for pictures than words, even when stimulus modality is switched so that the studied items are words and the test items are pictures. I have proposed (Paivio, 1976) that the effect reflects dual coding as well as perceptual properties of pictures that enhance item discriminability. Madigan (1983, p. 81) suggested, however, that the code-additivity hypothesis might not apply to recognition memory because he found higher recognition scores for pictures even when the pictures had been presented along with unrelated spoken words. Nonetheless, picture recognition was somewhat higher when the pictures had been accompanied by their names, and the general pattern of Madigan's picture/word results (1983, Fig. 3-3, p. 69) suggests additivity as well as picture superiority, much as in the free recall studies reviewed above. Dual coding could play a smaller role in recognition than in recall because pictures are less likely to activate a verbal code when subjects expect a recognition test than when they expect a recall test (Babbit, 1982). Recognition also appears to be relatively less dependent on elaborative processing (Paivio, 1976), although it can be augmented by elaboration (Wiseman, MacLeod, & Lootsteen, 1985). The contributions of dual coding and elaborative processing to recognition memory remain to be systematically studied using controlled coding procedures and incidental memory tests of the kind used with free recall.

## Synchronous Versus Sequential Organization

As indicated in the theoretical overview, the current version of DCT distinguishes between synchronous and sequential representational structures and processes. The term synchronous is partly equivalent to parallel processing but implies further that complex representational information can be integrated or unitized. This was the emphasis in the conceptual-peg experiments and related research by others on integrative imagery. Synchronous also implies that structural components are simultaneously available for processing, although the processing activity might be successive. A visual scene, for example, is perceptually available all at once, but optimal processing requires scanning of the components. The hallmark of simultaneous availability, however, is that the scanning is free from sequential constraints (i.e., it can go on in any order, Paivio, 1986, pp. 60-61). In DCT, visual imagery is assumed to be relatively free from sequential constraints and, at the same time, to be inefficient in sequential processing tasks unless augmented by verbal processes or sequentially constrained motor processes. The verbal system, on the other hand, is sequentially constrained and efficient in sequential processing tasks.

Freedom from sequential constraints was theoretically implicated in early research on associative symmetry in paired associate learning. A DCT prediction at that time (Paivio, 1971, pp. 278-285) was that symmetry would be found for picture pairs and concrete noun pairs because these could profit from the parallel processing capacity of the imagery system. Conversely, abstract pairs should not show associative symmetry because they would be processed mainly in the sequentially constrained verbal system. These predictions were supported most clearly by response latency data when recall was cued by either member of the studied pair: Response recall was slower with the right-hand member as the cue only in the case of abstract pairs (Smythe, 1970, summarized in Paivio, 1971, p. 284). The problem has not been systematically pursued in imagery research, perhaps because other issues related to imagery integration became more prominent.

The presumed sequential processing advantage of the verbal system has been investigated in a more sustained fashion. Paivio and Csapo (1969) tested the episodic memory implications of the assumption by comparing pictures, concrete words, and abstract words in memory span and serial learning as sequential memory tasks, and free recall and recognition memory as nonssequential item memory tasks. We varied the availability of verbal and imagery codes by presenting lists of items at either a fast rate designed to prevent covert naming of the pictures and a slower rate at which they could be named. Both rates allowed pictures to be identified and words to be read. The reasoning was that sequential memory depended on the availability of the verbal code, so that pictures would be inferior to words in the sequential tasks at the fast rate but not the slower rate. In item memory, however, pictures should not be inferior to words at the fast rate because an imaginal trace would suffice for recognition as well as verbal recall, and pictures should be superior at the slower rate because the probability of dual coding (hence additive effects) would be greater for pictures than words. These predictions were generally supported by interactions involving task, item type, and presentation rate.

Similar patterns were expected and obtained for words differing in concreteness: Concrete and abstract words did not differ in any memory task at the fast rate, presumably because interitem intervals were too brief for image generation; at the slower rate, concrete words were superior to abstract words in free recall but not in sequential memory, presumably because imagery, though accessible, was useful only in item recall.

We confirmed the picture/word results using recency discrimination and serial reconstruction as sequential memory tasks that do not require verbal responding (Paivio, 1971, p. 237). The rationale and results were also extended to auditory verbal and nonverbal stimuli (e.g., Paivio, Philipchalk, & Rowe, 1975). We found specifically that environmental sounds (of a telephone, train, etc.) were inferior to words in serial recall but not in free recall. Confirmatory results were also found by del Castillo and Gumenik (1972) using forms that varied in familiarity and nameability.

Other investigators, using various procedures, have provided evidence consistent with both the synchronous and sequential organizational assumptions of DCT (e.g., Peterson, 1975; Santa, 1977; Snodgrass, Burns, & Pirone, 1978). Santa (1977), for example, concluded from his results that verbal representations are sequentially constrained but not bound by the spatial properties of the stimulus, whereas representations of geometric forms preserve spatial relations among the components and are less constrained by sequential ordering.

Inconsistencies have also arisen, however, and these are yet to be resolved. In some studies, the supportive results have been relatively subtle or only partly consistent with the DCT assumptions (e.g., R.E. Anderson, 1976; Healy, 1977; O'Connor & Hermelin, 1972). Moreover, using procedures similar to those that Csapo and I had used, Lieury and Calvez (1986) obtained results that appear to contradict DCT in that pictures were not inferior and were sometimes recalled better than words in serial recall as well as free recall, even at fast presentation rates. Their procedures were not identical to ours, however, and the reason for the different picture/word patterns are therefore unclear. They also did not compare concrete and abstract words, and so, that aspect of our supportive results remains unchallenged.

## Extensions of the DCT Programme

The DCT processes used in the analysis of memory for words and word pairs generalized readily to larger units and structures (e.g., sentences) and to the problems of comprehension and production (Paivio, 1971, chap. 12 & 13; 1986, chap. 10; Paivio & Begg, 1981). Moreover, because of the historical emphasis on imagery in literary analysis of metaphor, figurative language became a natural target for DCT (Katz, Paivio, Marschark, & Clark, 1988; Paivio, 1979; Paivio & Walsh, in press). The theory has also been extended to a variety of other general domains such as bilingualism, semantic memory, neuropsychology, and individual differences in cognitive abilities and habits of thought. These have been reviewed comprehensively elsewhere (Paivio, 1986). In this section, I review the extensions to bilingualism, semantic memory, and neuropsychology in sufficient detail to show that the analyses and predictions were based entirely on applications of the DCT representational structures and processes of DCT, elaborated to accommodate the complexity of the phenomena. Further extensions are considered in the final section, in which I evaluate the current status of DCT.

A Bilingual Version of DCT: The language research included an important extension of DCT to bilingual memory and cognition (Paivio & Desrochers, 1980). The theoretical expansion began incidentally with an attempt to determine the relative contributions of imaginal and verbal codes in verbal memory tasks. The early results left open the possibility that the image superiority effects resulted entirely from uncontrolled dual coding. For example, pictures might be named covertly even if the task didn't require naming. One way to test the alternatives was to compare the effects of imaginal/verbal dual coding with a comparable verbal/verbal dual coding condition. This was achieved using bilingual subjects and bilingual translation equivalents as memory targets in the Paivio and Lambert (1981) experiments described earlier, which confirmed the image (and picture) superiority hypothesis and suggested, at the same time, that bilingual translation equivalents are functionally independent and additive in episodic memory.

Desrochers and I generalized those findings and others into a bilingual version of DCT, which included added assumptions concerning functional connections between language systems. One is that the two verbal systems that underlie  $L_{1}$  and  $L_{2}$  can be directly interconnected in varying degrees, depending on how often the bilingual has experienced direct translation during language acquisition. The between-language interconnections are assumed to differ from within-language interconnections only in that the bilingual ones are more constrained, less uncertain, than those within languages. For example, there are fewer alternative associations in the case of boy-garçon than alternative associations in the case of boy-girl. Both verbal systems are also assumed to be functionally connected to the nonverbal system so that they share common imagens or are connected to different ones, depending on the acquisition contexts for the two languages. The implications of these and other assumptions for issues related to bilingual cognition and second-language learning are reviewed in detail elsewhere (Paivio, 1986, chap. 11; Paivio & Desrochers, 1980). The main point here is to highlight the generative potential of DCT, which not only produced new observations but led to its own expansion as well.

Semantic Memory Tasks: Many semantic memory tasks depend on sensorimotor information in cognitive representations. Dual coding theory is fundamentally concerned with semantic memory in that sense, and performance on all of the tasks considered up to this point depends on relevant semantic memory properties (imagery value, verbal associative meaningfulness, etc). This section focuses on symbolic comparison studies because they reveal especially clearly the detailed nature of the information available in cognitive representations. The DCT experiments were prompted by a study by Moyer (1973) in which subjects were asked to indicate which of two animal names referred to the larger or smaller animal. The reaction time for the decision varied inversely with the magnitude of the difference in animal size. For example, dog was chosen more quickly as the larger member when paired with frog than with cat. This symbolic distance effect is similar to what has been observed with perceptual stimuli that differ in magnitude.

Moyer's (1973) psychophysical paradigm seemed ideal for studying structural and functional differences between imaginal and verbal representations. For example, pictures and words can be used in symbolic comparisons involving nonverbal and verbal attributes that are not perceptually apparent in the stimuli (e.g., pictured objects can be equated on physical size). Dual coding theory implies that comparisons should be faster with pictures than words when the symbolic attribute is an intrinsic property of the referent object, such as size, because the nonverbal representations that contain such information are more directly activated by pictures. Conversely, the decisions should be relatively faster with words in the case of such linguistic attributes as the familiarity or pronounceability of the name of the object. These predictions were confirmed for object size and name pronounceability comparisons of printed word pairs and picture pairs that did not differ in physical size. Later experiments supported similar DCT predictions involving such concrete attributes as object shape and weight and the angular separation of the hands of mental clocks that represent different times (for a summary, see Paivio, 1986, chap. 9).

More complex interactions were also predicted from DCT (Paivio, 1975b). Particularly striking was a Stroop-like interference effect on symbolic size comparison time with pictures as stimuli when perceptual and memory size differences were incongruent. For example, subjects were relatively slow to choose zebra as the larger object when it was depicted as smaller than the comparison object, lamp. Conversely, facilitation occurred when perceptual and memory size were congruent (e.g., the zebra shown as larger than the lamp). The interference effect did not occur with printed words that differed in physical size. A further experiment showed that the interaction was reversed when subjects were asked to choose the pictured object that looked farther away: The comparisons were now faster under the size-incongruent condition than the size-congruent condition. The contrasting effects are especially important theoretically because both size and relative distance judgements must be based on memory size. Thus the same mental representations can mediate different behavioural outcomes, depending on task demands.

The DCT approach to symbolic comparisons has also encountered some obstacles. Common coding theorists (e.g., Potter & Faulconer, 1975; Seymour, 1973; Snodgrass, 1984) have argued that the picture RT advantage in tasks involving semantic decisions, such as deciding the category of a pictured or named object, can be explained by assuming that pictures access abstract semantic information more quickly than words. This ad hoc assumption would account for the picture advantage in symbolic comparisons. However, it would not so readily explain other effects predicted from DCT, including faster comparisons with words on certain attributes, correlations between individual differences in symbolic comparisons and relevant cognitive abilities (Paivio, 1986, pp. 185-187), and the Stroop-like interference effect with size-incongruent pictures.

A specific empirical critique by Foltz, Poltrock, and Potts (1984) is more problematic: Although they found, as I did, that the size-incongruity effect did not occur with printed words as stimuli when the same pairs were repeated a number of times in the list, they did obtain the incongruity effect when each word pair was presented only once. Their findings modify my conclusions but do not obviate the picture/word difference in the size congruity effect because they did not include any direct comparisons of pictures and words. Moreover, Foltz et al. did not comment on my finding, crucial to DCT, that the size-congruity effect with pictures was reversed when subjects were asked to indicate which of the two pictured objects looked farther away. Up to this point, then, DCT remains the best account of the total range of findings from symbolic size comparison experiments.

Comparisons involving other symbolic attributes have also produced some surprises. Comparisons turned out to be faster with pictures than words in the case of such abstract attributes as pleasantness and monetary value of referent objects, which on the face of it seems inconsistent with DCT. Even in those cases, however, one must access representations of the referent objects in order to make decisions about their pleasantness or value, and such access is more direct with pictures. More puzzling was the absence of picture/word differences in comparisons of colour attributes of things (Paivio & te Linde, 1980) — puzzling, in that we expected comparisons to be faster with line drawings than words because colour is a concrete perceptual attribute. We offered a tentative explanation based on neuropsychological and perceptual information which also suggests that colour is cognitively unique; however, as yet the problem has not been empirically resolved and so remains anomalous in the generally successful DCT account of symbolic comparisons.

Neuropsychology and DCT: The assumptions of DCT were based originally on behavioural data independent of neuropsychological considerations. Nonetheless, neuropsychological data are relevant to DCT, and I have taken account of them over the years primarily as further convergent evidence for aspects of the theory. For example, some of our early research focussed on pupillary reactions and other psychophysiological measures as possible correlates of imagery and verbal processes (for a review see Paivio, 1973). Conversely, the theory can serve as a basis for organizing and making predictions about neuropsychological data. Here, I will be concerned mainly with what the data have to say about DCT. The evidence has been reviewed comprehensively elsewhere (Paivio, 1986, 1989; Paivio & Begg, 1981, chap. 15; Paivio & te Linde, 1982), and so I only summarize the most pertinent points. The main sources of evidence are dysfunctions resulting from localized brain lesions and surgical separation of the two cerebral hemispheres, as well as functional asymmetries revealed by lateralized presentation of stimuli to the ears or eyes of normal and brain damaged subjects. The available evidence from these sources is remarkably consistent with the general assumptions of DCT.

The verbal/nonverbal symbolic distinction is supported by the general finding that verbal and nonverbal processing modes are dependent on different parts of the brain. The left hemisphere of most people controls speech and is more efficient than the right hemisphere at processing verbal material in such tasks as perceptual recognition, episodic memory, and comprehension. The right hemisphere has the advantage in such nonverbal tasks as face identification and discrimination, recognition of nonverbal sounds, and memory for faces and spatial patterns. The generalization holds for different sensory modalities, justifying the conclusions that the distinction is a verbal/nonverbal symbolic one that cuts across sensory modalities.

The assumption of orthogonal symbolic and sensorimotor systems is likewise supported by neurological data that show functional dissociations of the two dimensions. For example, whereas printed names are usually recognized better when presented to the left hemisphere, pictures of familiar objects are recognized equally easily when presented to either hemisphere (more about that below). Thus, the pattern of asymmetries varies with the verbal/nonverbal symbolic distinction when the sensory input modality is visual in both cases. A contrasting pattern emerges when pictures are compared with nonverbal sounds: Whereas pictures show hemispheric symmetry, such environmental sounds as a car starting are recognized better in the left car, hence the right hemisphere. In this contrast, the pattern of hemispheric asymmetries varies with sensory modality (auditory vs. visual) when the symbolic modality of the material is nonverbal in each case. Selective deficits resulting from brain damage provide further evidence of such dissociations. In pure alexia, for example, damage to posterior regions of the left hemisphere results in an inability to process visual language while speech recognition remains intact. Thus, the functional deficit is again related to sensory modality when symbolic modality remains constant, in this case verbal.

The DCT distinction between representational, referential, and associative connections and processes is also generally supported, with special implications for the hemispheric location of imagery functions. Paivio and Ernest (1971) reported a perceptual recognition experiment that bears on representational processing. Letters, familiar pictures, or geometric forms were flashed tachistoscopically to right- or left-visual fields at brief durations. The novel feature of the study was that the subjects were divided into high- and low-visual-imagery ability groups on the basis of their combined scores on spatial manipulation and questionnaire tests. The results were as follows: High-ability subjects were far superior to low-ability subjects in recognition of pictures presented to either field. Field had no effect, and an interaction trend was not significant. These data suggest that the representations necessary for recognition of visual objects are equally available in both hemispheres and that they are more available or accessible for subjects who are high rather than low in visual imagery ability. Thus, both hemispheres apparently have the representations and processing skills required for generating mental images of familiar objects.

The functional symmetry is especially significant because it occurred despite the fact that the subjects indicated their recognition by naming the pictures, which is a verbal referential task that might be expected to favour the left hemisphere. Since this did not happen, it must mean that recognition performance was dependent mainly on the availability of nonverbal representations. That inference was confirmed by the absence of field effects in picture identification using a matching task that did not require naming (Ernest, 1983). Other data in the literature also support the generalization that representations for familiar objects are available in both hemispheres. Our results showed quite different hemispheric patterns for letters and forms. For example, letters were recognized better in the right field than in the left by both groups of subjects, which did not differ in recognition performance.

The neuropsychological literature is rich in information that implicates referential processes. The anomias generally show that damage to the left hemisphere can result in naming difficulties without similarly affecting recognition of the visual objects themselves or production of their names in reading or verbal associative tasks. Such findings indicate that verbal referential processing skills have been impaired while verbal representational and associative processes remain intact. Other studies similarly implicate referential imagery pathways. For example, Beauvois and Saillant (1985) described a brain-damaged patient with visual anomia for colours. The patient performed normally on tests that could be answered entirely on the basis of verbal knowledge or entirely on the basis of visual knowledge. Her performance was drastically impaired, however, when required to switch symbolic modes by naming presented colours or by pointing to a colour when asked to do so. For example, she could not respond correctly to the request, “Show me what colour a cherry is.”

The referential processing impairment was also revealed by purely verbal tests in which the response depended on imagery. For example, after being instructed to “imagine a beautiful snowy landscape,” the patient could not answer the question, “Tell me what colour snow is,” although she was able to do so when asked “What do people say when they are asked what colour snow is?” Since the colour aphasia was associated with a left-hemisphere lesion that extended from the occipital to the inferior temporal lobe, we can conclude that the left hemisphere dominates in referential processing tasks in which mental images must be generated to words or described verbally. This conclusion is generally consistent with Kosslyn’s (e.g., Kosslyn, Holtzman, Farah, & Gazzaniga, 1985) and Farah’s (e.g., 1984) theory of left-hemisphere image generation. However, their hypothesis is restricted to multipart rather than global images of objects, and it also does not exclude the possibility of image generation via nonverbal associative processing in either hemisphere (e.g., right-hemisphere generation of visual images to environmental sounds), as would be allowed by DCT. Sergent’s (1990) recent review of the relevant evidence also led her to reject a strong left-hemisphere hypothesis and to conclude that both hemispheres contribute simultaneously and jointly to visual image generation.

The differential capacities of verbal and nonverbal systems for sequential and synchronous processing also have neuropsychological support. Studies with brain-damaged and normal subjects (e.g., Mills & Rollman, 1979, 1980) indicate that the left hemisphere, especially the left temporal lobe, dominates in tasks that require discrimination of temporal order, even in tasks that do not involve speech. The same structures are crucial in sequential organization of speech (e.g., Kimura, 1982). It seems, therefore, that the sequential processing skills of the verbal system depend on a more general temporal processing capacity of motor and acoustic systems for which the left hemisphere has become specialized. The converse synchronous processing capacity of the nonverbal system is reflected most clearly in right-hemisphere superiority in tasks that require cognitive organization and transformation of complex patterns, as in perceptual closure, mental rotation, and other tests of spatial transformation ability.

This completes the retrospective review of the development of DCT and the empirical issues most closely associated with it. We turn next to a brief evaluation of the class of theories that currently is the major alternative to DCT and other theories that stress the modality specificity of cognitive representations.

## COMPUTATIONAL CRITIQUE OF DCT

I mentioned earlier that the verbal mediational alternative to imagery was replaced by a computational analogue in which the language of mind became the logical proposition (c.g., Pylyshyn, 1973). The approach encompassed language as well, so that, at the “deep” representational level, all perceptual and behavioural knowledge was reduced conceptually to a common, amodal form. The alternative was widely adopted in cognitive theories (c.g., J.R. Anderson & Bower, 1973; Palmer, 1975; Simon, 1972) and explicitly contrasted with DCT: “Unlike the dual-code position, there is no fundamental difference in how perceptually based and verbally based information is represented in memory” (Kieras, 1978, pp. 533-534). Logical and empirical counterarguments (c.g., Kosslyn & Pomerantz, 1977; Paivio, 1977) were followed by the emergence of theoretical compromises. J.R. Anderson (1978) compared a theory he identified as “basically the dual-coding model of Paivio (1971)” with propositional theories and concluded that the controversy is at an impasse because either type of model can be constructed to accommodate existing data. Kosslyn and his collaborators (c.g., Kosslyn, 1980; Kosslyn, Pinker, Smith, & Schwartz, 1979) compromised by proposing a computer simulation model in which propositional descriptions generate nonpropositional mental images that can be scanned, rotated, and otherwise operated on by various processing components in the system.

The upshot of the imagery debates was that single-code approaches generally lost their popularity and were replaced by a variety of triple coding systems that include imagery, language, and a propositional interface. They could be described as propositionally augmented dual coding theories. J.R. Anderson (1983) explicitly adopted such a tri-codal approach to the representation of knowledge in order to accommodate functional distinctions that could not be handled by single-code theories. Others (e.g., Potter & Faulconer, 1975; Seymour, 1973; Snodgrass, 1984; Theios & Amrhein, 1989) have proposed more specific triple-coding models to account for results of studies concerned with picture and word processing. The new brand of computational theory called connectionism, so prominent now in cognitive psychology and cognitive science, is beginning to be applied to visual representation and imagery problems (e.g., Mel, 1986), but its potential for handling a wider range of such phenomena remains to be demonstrated.

Elsewhere I have presented detailed analyses of logical and explanatory problems with formal propositional approaches (Paivio, 1986, chap. 1 & 3). It should be apparent by now, however, that I prefer to use empirical criteria to judge the adequacy of scientific theories, and I believe that the evidence shows that DCT has more than held its own relative to the propositional alternatives up to the time of my 1986 update of the theory. The remainder of this review evaluates the current status of DCT in light of criticisms and research findings that have appeared since 1986.

## CURRENT STATUS OF DCT

The first part of this update deals with two of the earliest and most basic issues in DCT research, namely, word concreteness effects and picture versus word effects in memory tasks. The second part concerns extensions of DCT to issues not dealt with up to this point, namely, DCT as an alternative to recent schema theories of memory and comprehension and applications of DCT to problems in philosophy of science.

## Concreteness Effects in Memory Tasks

The DCT interpretation of concreteness effects relies particularly on the role of imagery as an integrative mechanism in associative memory and as an additive supplement to the verbal code in item memory. Criticisms of imagery integration began with our own research (Marschark & Paivio, 1977) on cued recall of concrete and abstract sentences. We found that recall was generally higher for concrete than for abstract sentences but, unexpectedly, the two types of sentences did not differ in the extent to which recall was integrated, as measured by the retrieval effectiveness of verbal cues that were related to the meaning of the whole sentences as compared to cues that were related only to one of the content words in each sentence. The higher overall recall for concrete sentences accords with the additivity hypothesis of DCT, but the equivalent integration effect is inconsistent with the view that imagery is a superior integrative medium. Inasmuch as subjects reported using imagery often to remember concrete sentences but rarely to remember abstract ones, and the image frequencies correlated highly (.61 and .82 in two experiments) with recall, Marschark and I concluded that dual coding adequately accounts for the general recall superiority of concrete sentences but that some common representational mechanism is needed to explain the equivalent integration effects. Verbal association processes are a logical DCT candidate for such a mechanism because they apply equally to concrete and abstract language. Another candidate is some amodal form of representation or process, and Marschark (e.g., Marschark & Hunt, 1989) has pursued that alternative in the context of a general critique of DCT. I deal first with the resolution of the integration issue and then with the Marschark and Hunt critique.

The question to be answered in regard to integration is as follows: Why was cued recall equally integrative for concrete and abstract sentences, while at the same time overall recall was higher for the concrete sentences and correlated substantially with reported imagery use during recall? Taking account of the verbal processing mechanisms of DCT, Mary Walsh and I (Paivio & Walsh, 1991) reasoned that integrative recall can be produced by strong verbal associative relations as well as by imagery. Thus, abstract pairs can be recalled integratively if they are associatively related, whereas concrete pairs can be integrated using imagery even when associative relations are weak. We tested the hypothesis using free and cued recall of concrete and abstract word pairs that varied in associative relatedness, and we measured integration by the increment from free to cued recall (cf. Begg, 1972). These variables had not been included together in any single prior experiment. Kusyszyn and Paivio (1966) found concreteness and relatedness effects but used only cued recall. Begg (1972) found a greater free-to-cued recall increment for concrete than abstract pairs, but used only weakly related pairs. Marschark and Paivio (1977) did not vary relatedness or type of test. Paivio, Clark, and Khan (1988) varied pair relatedness and concreteness but only in cued recall. Marschark and Hunt (1989) included the three variables in different experiments but not together in a single experiment so that free and cued recall could be appropriately compared.

Our general procedure was to present a list of pairs followed either by cued recall of the response members or free recall of the pairs. In the case of the latter, we scored recall of response members so that the recall targets would be the same in free and cued recall (cf. Marschark & Hunt, 1989). Our lists included equal numbers of associatively related and unrelated concrete pairs and abstract pairs. Different subjects received free and cued recall tests.

The results agreed with our expectations from DCT. An overall analysis revealed significant main effects of concreteness, relatedness, and recall task, as well as a triple interaction. The integration effect, as measured by the increment from free to cued recall, was large and statistically equivalent for concrete related pairs (39% increment), abstract related pairs (32%), and concrete unrelated pairs (31%) and was much lower for abstract unrelated pairs (11%). Thus, strong verbal associations were necessary to produce integrative recall when imagery was relatively unavailable as an integrative mechanism (abstract pairs). Conversely, when imagery was available (concrete pairs), recall was highly integrative even when verbal associations were weak.

Postexperimental strategy reports provided convergent evidence for the DCT interpretations: (a) Imagery was reported much more often than verbal strategies for concrete items, whereas verbal strategies predominated in the case of abstract items; and (b) imagery frequency correlated positively and significantly with both free and cued recall of both concrete and abstract items (rs ranged from .33 to .57), whereas verbal strategies correlated mainly with cued recall and more so for abstract pairs (r = .80) than concrete pairs (.32). Thus, consistent with DCT, both imaginal and verbal strategies contributed to recall and did so more strongly in cued recall, where their integrative function was most effective.

Returning to the original issue, our data suggest that the equivalent integrative recall of concrete and abstract sentences reported by Marschark and Paivio (1977) was due to the strong verbal associative relations between retrieval cues and recall targets in both kinds of sentences. A re-examination of the recall pattern in their Experiment 1 (Table 1, p. 221) supports our interpretation: The proportions of target words recalled, conditionalized on original learning, were high for concrete sentences when the retrieval cues were congruent (.94) and when they were incongruent (.81) with the recall targets. For abstract sentences, proportionate recall was substantial with congruent cues (.62) but not with incongruent cues (.18). The interaction of concreteness by cue type was significant, and its form suggests that, when imagery was available as a mediator, recall was high even when the retrieval cues were associatively unrelated to the targets, whereas when imagery was unavailable, recall was high only when cues and targets were related. This DCT interpretation, yet to be independently tested with sentences, provides a principled account of data that were puzzling when viewed only from the imagery perspective of DCT without taking full advantage of the explanatory potential of verbal associative processes.

I turn now to Marschark and Hunt's (1989) critique of DCT. They proposed that memory performance depends on activation of relational and distinctive information about items to be remembered. Distinctiveness applies to specific item information as well as the relational information that links items to each other. Both kinds of information can involve any dimension, including perceptual information derived from imaginal processing. This relational/distinctive processing framework is asserted to be superior to DCT because it explains findings pertaining to concreteness and imagery effects that are inconsistent with DCT as well as ones that are consistent with it.

The critique and relevant evidence warrant a more detailed response but a summary will do for present purposes. One point is that the relational/distinctiveness framework has been applied inconsistently over studies, so that activated relational information purportedly accounts for both the absence of concreteness effects in sentence memory (Marschark, 1985) and the occurrence of concreteness effects in cued (and free) recall of word pairs (Marschark & Hunt, 1989). How they resolve the apparent inconsistency remains to be seen.

A second counterargument is that relational and distinctive processing assumptions have long been associated with DCT and, therefore, they do not suffice as a basis for distinguishing between the two approaches. The pertinent evidence has already been reviewed earlier, and I simply remind the reader of the experiment by Rowe and Paivio (1971) in which imagery-based distinctiveness and relational functions were operationally distinguished: Only item-specific imagery facilitated verbal discrimination learning, whereas compound (relational) imagery facilitated incidental cued recall following verbal discrimination learning.

There seems to be a clear difference between Marschark and Hunt's (1989) approach and DCT only with respect to the item-specific function of imagery: They assume (Exp. 3) that imagery information associated with individual items serves only a distinctiveness function, which cannot enhance recall unless it is accompanied by relational processing, whereas DCT assumes that imagery can enhance recall of individual items through independent and additive effects of imaginal and verbal codes. Thus, unlike DCT, Marschark and Hunt predicted no effect of concreteness on free recall in the absence of relational processing.

Consistent with their prediction, they found nonsignificant concreteness effects on free recall of response members of paired associates in five independent comparisons over four experiments. Nonetheless, the small differences favoured concrete words in every case, so that the experiment-wise effect is significant by an exact test and is consistent with DCT. Walsh and I (Paivio & Walsh, 1991) confirmed the DCT prediction in the context of the integration experiment discussed above. Additivity of imaginal and verbal codes was generally supported by concrete superiority in both cued and free recall. Concreteness and relatedness interacted in the case of free recall, so that the recall advantage for concrete words was greater for related than unrelated pairs, but planned comparisons confirmed that the difference was significant even for the latter. Thus, our results showed that image availability increases item recall even when conditions are unfavourable for relational processing. These results add to the various kinds of support for the code additivity hypothesis reviewed earlier, including the telling observation that item concreteness facilitated free recall under conditions that encouraged image coding, but was attenuated or absent under conditions that interfered with imagery (e.g., Paivio & Csapo, 1969). I conclude that DCT still provides the best explanation for concreteness effects in memory tasks generally (for further recent evidence, see Nelson & Schreiber, in press).

## The Picture Superiority Effect and Implicit Versus Explicit Memory

Roediger and Weldon (1987; Weldon & Roediger, 1987) presented a new interpretation and supporting data concerning the picture superiority effect in recall, which they extended in principle to imagery effects. They emphasized retrieval processes, arguing that these had been relatively neglected while encoding and storage factors had been favoured in the DCT approach. I note in passing that the general charge is not justified inasmuch as the conceptual-peg hypothesis specifically concerns the retrieval functions of stimulus-evoked imagery in associative memory tasks. More importantly, however, Roediger and Weldon capitalized on more recent memory paradigms in which retrieval factors also are salient, namely, implicit as compared to explicit retention tests (Graf & Schachter, 1985) and a related distinction between data-driven and conceptually driven tasks (Jacoby, 1983). Implicit tests entail indirect measures of retention, usually by means of repetition priming whereby prior exposure to a stimulus facilitates accuracy or speed of responding to that stimulus or a portion of it (e.g., completing fragmented words) relative to an unprimed condition. Explicit tests are the traditional ones in which people are asked to recollect information. Conceptually driven tests (e.g., free recall) require subjects to produce their own retrieval cues, whereas data-driven tasks (e.g., perceptual identification, word fragment completion) provide the subject with relevant stimulus information during the test.

Roediger and Weldon (1987) found the usual picture superiority effect in free recall as well as in another conceptually driven task in which recall was cued by words that were associatively related to the studied items. Pictures were also remembered better than words in an implicit memory task in which the subjects were required to identify fragmented pictures, but words surpassed pictures when retention was measured by a word fragment completion test. They concluded (a) that the picture superiority effect can be reversed by changing retrieval demands, and (b) that conceptually driven retrieval tests generally result in better retention for pictures than words, whereas data-driven tests can produce better retention for either words or pictures, depending on the physical match between study and test stimuli. Roediger and Weldon then extended their analysis to other picture/word and imagery results in the literature and proposed a more general interpretation in terms of transfer-appropriate processing, in which the emphasis is on matching processes or procedures (cf. Kolers, 1973; Kolers & Roediger, 1984) rather than on mental structures or elements as in DCT or in sensory/semantic feature models (e.g., Nelson, 1979).

Let us examine Roediger and Weldon's (1987) conclusions and observations from a DCT perspective. The general points to notice are that (a) factors they emphasize have also been taken into account in dual coding research and theory, and (b) transfer-appropriate processing, though relevant, is insufficient to account for some findings that are consistent with DCT.

We have already seen the early emphasis on retrieval processes in the conceptual-peg hypothesis of DCT. A second point of contact is that associative factors play a greater role in free recall than in recognition memory performance (see Paivio, 1976), which accords with the distinction between conceptually driven and data-driven retention tests, with the qualification that recognition memory performance cannot be completely data driven because it is also affected by dual coding variables. Specifically, picture recognition improves when the pictures are accompanied by relevant names during study (see the earlier comments on Madigan's, 1983, results), and concrete, high-imagery words are recognized better than abstract words (Olver, 1965).

A more complete theoretical and empirical parallel is found in the DCT distinction between representational, referential, and associative interconnections and processes. Recall that representational processing refers to the activation of internal representations by structurally similar stimulus patterns — logogens by words or other unitized verbal patterns and imagens by familiar objects or pictures. Referential processing refers to the cross-system activation of imagens by referentially related logogens or vice versa. Associative processing refers to spread of activation within systems, as in word associations or spread of imagery from one object to another. The relevant point here is that representation processing is data driven by definition, whereas referential and associative processing are conceptual, again by definition. These theoretical distinctions are associated with corresponding empirical procedures or defining operations. For example, representation processing is operationalized, by stimulus identification tests such as tachistoscopic recognition, referential, by imagery or naming reaction times, and associative, by word association or analogous (rarely studied) imaginal association times.

Paivio and O'Neill (1970) investigated implications of the DCT analysis using tachistoscopic identification of words that varied in familiarity/frequency, imagery value, and verbal associative meaningfulness (m). They reasoned that case of identification should depend primarily on the availability of representations corresponding to the target word. Word frequency and familiarity presumably reflect representation availability and so should be related to word identification. Imagery value measures the availability of referent images, and m measures the availability of verbal associates to the target word; thus the word must be identified before it can activate images or verbal associates. The results were generally as predicted: Identification was strongly related to familiarity, unrelated to imagery value, and weakly, though significantly, related to m (the basis of the slight m effect remains unclear). The familiarity and imagery results have been confirmed by others (e.g., Boles, 1983). The pattern contrasts sharply with memory studies where, as we have already seen, imagery value surpasses familiarity as a predictor of word recall. The important point is that the results and DCT interpretation map onto the distinction between data-driven tasks (in this case, word identification) and conceptually driven tasks (word recall). Performance on the data-driven task depends on a variable (familiarity) that affects pattern matching between the word and its mental representation, whereas performance on the conceptually driven task depends on a variable (imagery) that facilitates retrieval of the word from memory.

The interpretation applies directly to the memory priming effects reported by Roodiger and Weldon (1987) for pictures and words using fragmented pictures and words as test stimuli. The effects entailed representational level (data-driven) processing, with fragmented pictures more easily reactivating imagens that had been previously activated by the complete pictures and with word fragments more easily reactivating visual word representations (logogens) previously activated by the complete words. Conceptually driven referential processing was also required in the case of pictures because the fragments were identified by naming, but the naming response could occur only if the appropriate imagen had already been activated by the picture fragment. Elsewhere (Paivio, 1986, p. 103), I have discussed similar interpretive complications created by naming when mutilated word and mutilated picture tests are used to measure representational processing ability.

The dissociation of picture and word fragments in the implicit memory tests clearly demonstrates transfer-appropriate processing in the Roediger and Weldon sense. That interpretation is also consistent with DCT in the more specific sense that prior representational-level processing transfers positively to a test trial that also implicates representational processing. However, transfer-appropriate processing does not easily explain other DCT-relevant results already mentioned earlier. One such finding was picture superiority in recognition memory even when the test stimuli were words. I had pointed out (Paivio, 1976, p. 128) that the effect appears to be inconsistent with the encoding specificity principle, which implicates transfer-appropriate processing. The processing interpretation would work only if we assume that concrete words evoked images during study and that those images facilitated picture recognition because of a representational picture/image match or because of a study/test dual-coding match involving both verbal and imaginal codes.

The Paivio and Lambert (1981) results also are incompatible with any direct application of the transfer-specific processing interpretation. Thus, recall was better for English words generated to pictures (naming) than to French words (translation). This could be explained only by transfer-appropriate processing if we also assume (ad hoc) that picture-name processing (presumably transferring to image-name processing during recall) is more memorable than translation processing. The DCT explanation is simpler and not ad hoc, since it follows directly from the code-additivity hypothesis coupled with the empirically derived picture (or image) superiority hypothesis. The interpretive advantage of DCT applies as well to the superior recall of stimulus words that had been imaged as compared with ones that had been translated.

The above examples illustrate the parallels between DCT processes and the various distinctions that have arisen from the recent implicit memory research, and they suggest, too, that the structural and processing assumptions of DCT have some explanatory value in that context. In making those comparisons, I am not claiming that DCT either predicts or accounts for the novel memory findings that have resulted from the discovery of implicit (or priming) memory and the subsequent attempts to dissociate it from explicit memory. However, neither did any other theory predict the initial observations, and the post hoc category distinctions (explicit vs. implicit, conceptually driven vs. data-driven) have not yet led to a satisfactory theoretical account of the empirical phenomena.

## DCT as an Alternative to Schema Theories

Schema theories in their various forms (scripts, frames, story grammars) are currently popular in many areas of psychology, especially text memory and comprehension. Such theories have been criticized for their vagueness concerning the nature of schematic representations, for the difficulty of deriving clear predictions from them, and for such data as accurate retention of details, which seems inconsistent with the basic schema assumption that details are reconstructed from the abstract base (e.g., see Alba & Hasher, 1983). For such reasons and the general point that schema theories rely on propositional representations, I proposed a dual coding alternative (Paivio, 1986, pp. 224-227) and suggested how it could be applied to the problem of text processing. This was later developed more fully (Sadoski, Paivio, & Goetz, in press) as an alternative to schema theories of reading.

A principal aim of theories of text processing is to characterize the thematic organization structure and the processing mechanisms that would account for comprehension and memory phenomena. The propositional schema model of van Dijk and Kintsch (1983) does so in terms of a system of semantically interrelated propositions that define the text's coherence. Their general model also includes verbatim surface representations and a situational model that the hearer or reader constructs about the situation described by the text. Thus, we have here another version of triple coding theories, which includes components that parallel DCT, connected to a propositional representational base. However, unlike the imagery system in DCT, the situational model also turns out to have a propositional format.

The DCT alternative relies entirely on properties of the verbal and imaginal systems to characterize text structure. Verbal/contextual and associative relations (measured by associative overlap scores) would define text cohesiveness, together with common referential images evoked by co-referential terms, synonyms, and so on. Episodes and the overall theme could be represented both verbally and imaginally: verbally, by the terms episode and theme or their close verbal associates; imaginally, by repeated imaginal contexts; and so on. Text processing would consist of representational, referential, and associative processes operating over the verbal and imaginal structure. It can readily be seen that this is a straightforward extension of such concepts as verbal and imaginal integration previously applied to paired-associate and list-learning problems.

Specific aspects of the DCT approach have direct empirical support. Khan and Paivio (1988) showed that verbal associations can account for memory effects that Rabinowitz and Mandler (1983) had previously attributed to schema. Sadoski (1983) obtained story comprehension, memory, and imagery report data from elementary school children concerning a story they had read aloud. Analysis of the data revealed, among other things, that general verbal and imagery factors accounted well for comprehension and recall scores. The imagery factor, in particular, correlated with recall scores for the theme, events, and plot of the story, suggesting to Sadoski that imagery was the theme factor that represents the essential meaning of the story. Sadoski concluded generally that his results were consistent with specific assumptions and hypotheses of DCT as these had been applied to literary analysis (Paivio, 1983). The theory remains to be tested more comprehensively in the context of text processing.

## Application of DCT to Problems in Philosophy of Science

Philosophers of science have traditionally sought to understand the nature of science using speculative, rationalistic methods. One of the issues on which they focussed is the distinction between observational (O) and theoretical (T) terms. This O/T distinction was the foundation of logical positivism, whose proponents argued for the validity of the distinction and drew up lists of attributes that distinguished the meanings of the two classes of terms. For example, observational terms (e.g., tree) are noninferential in that their referents can be observed directly, whereas theoretical terms (e.g., mass) are inferential, not directly observable. Opponents of this view argued that the distinction cannot be drawn sharply because all terms, including so-called observational ones, are equally theory laden in that their meanings depend on the conceptual biases of the user. Clark and I (Clark & Paivio, 1989) argued that the O/T issue is basically psychological, in that it has to do with the thought processes of scientists and that it can be resolved only by theoretically guided empirical procedures.

Accordingly, Clark and I (1989) tested the implications of DCT for the O/T issue, hypothesizing that O/T entails a domain-specific (scientific language) variant of the concreteness/abstractness dimension, implicating imagery and dual coding as key processes. We used psychology as the target science because its many subareas provide a rich scientific vocabulary that seems to vary widely on the O/T dimension. Different groups of psychologists rated a sample of scientific terms on observability, theoretical status, concreteness, and a number of other semantic dimensions that some philosophers view as important correlates of O/T. We found that O/T is a highly reliable dimension that correlated over .90 with concreteness and other conceptually relevant (but not irrelevant) attributes of the terms. An experiment showed, moreover, that imagery RT to the terms increased linearly with their observability, whereas verbal-associative RT did not. These results agree fully with DCT-derived predictions and prior studies using non-scientific language, some of which I have described earlier in this article.

Our results also happen to be consistent with the views of philosophical proponents of the O/T distinction, especially those with an empiricist rather than a rationalist approach to the philosophy of science. This would include the logical positivists who gave us the O/T distinction, but our support for the O/T distinction does not imply agreement with the general positivistic programme, especially their attempt to develop a completely axiomatic science of science. That goal puts the emphasis on internal consistency as the main criterion for scientific theory, much as in current computational approaches to cognitive science. Such approaches fall short of their goal because observable phenomena are too variable to be pulled into the axiomatic (computational) domain. Dual coding theory has its own shortcomings, but it avoids that rationalist strait-jacket because it is empirically based.
