# Situation Awareness,Mental Workload,and Trust in Automation:Viable,Empirically Supported Cognitive Engineering Constructs

**Raja Parasuraman**

George Mason University

**Thomas B. Sheridan**

Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Department of Transportation Volpe Center

**Christopher D. Wickens**

Alion Science and Technology, Micro Analysis & Design Division

**ABSTRACT:** Cognitive engineering needs viable constructs and principles to promote better understanding and prediction of human performance in complex systems. Three human cognition and performance constructs that have been the subjects of much attention in research and practice over the past three decades are situation awareness (SA), mental workload, and trust in automation. Recently, Dekker and Woods (2002) and Dekker and Hollnagel (2004; henceforth DWH) argued that these constructs represent “folk models” without strong empirical foundations and lacking scientific status. We counter this view by presenting a brief description of the large science base of empirical studies on these constructs. We show that the constructs can be operationalized using behavioral, physiological, and subjective measures, supplemented by computational modeling, but that the constructs are also distinct from human performance. DWH also caricatured as “abracadabra” a framework suggested by us to address the problem of the design of automated systems (Parasuraman, Sheridan, & Wickens, 2000). We point to several factual and conceptual errors in their description of our approach. Finally, we rebut DWH’s view that SA, mental workload, and trust represent folk concepts that are not falsifiable. We conclude that SA, mental workload, and trust are viable constructs that are valuable in understanding and predicting human-system performance in complex systems.

## Introduction

COGNITIVE ENGINEERING AND, MORE GENERALLY, HUMAN FACTORS/ERGONOMICS (HF/E) depend on the development of viable constructs and principles to promote better understanding of human performance in complex systems. HF/E research puts forward theoretically based constructs and tests their generality through empirical studies in a wide variety of laboratory tasks, simulators, “microworlds,” and actual work domains. A desirable feature is that the constructs and/or principles are quantifiable in the form of either mathematical or computational models. At the same time, HF/E practice is concerned with the judicious application of general principles identified through this kind of research to the design of systems to support effective, safe, and enjoyable human work. Some early examples of empirically supported constructs and general principles that have enjoyed success according to these criteria include stimulus-response compatibility, Fitts’ law, visual search models, quantification of working memory capacity, and the crossover model of tracking performance (Fitts, 1951; Jagasinski & Flach, 2006; Proctor & van Zandt, 1984; Wickens & Hollands, 2000).

In this article, we discuss three more recent human performance constructs that have been the object of much attention in cognitive engineering research and practice over the past three decades: situation awareness (SA), mental workload, and trust in automation. We chose to reexamine these areas for three reasons. First, there is now an extensive, mature record of empirical research that has deep ened scientific knowledge and understanding of these human performance areas. The time is therefore right to take stock, to examine whether these constructs are worthwhile, and to see what has been learned. We do not attempt a comprehen sive review but instead summarize the main points of each. We provide evidence on the scientific status of these three human performance constructs and argue that they are viable and valuable for use in HF/E practice.

Second, SA, workload, and trust often feature in attempts to solve an old prob lem in HF/E: function allocation betweens humans and automated systems (Fitts, 1951; Jordan, 1963; see Gaines, McCarthy, Fallon, & Bannon, 2000, for a recent review). Again, we do not review the many attempts (some failed, some more successful) to solve the function allocation issue. Rather, we discuss to what extent an evaluation of SA, workload, and trust is relevant to the design of automated systems.

A third reason for our examination is that these and related questions were discussed in two recent articles by Dekker and colleagues. The issues were raised in the context of an attack on the viability of these HF/E constructs and their use in methods for function allocation between humans and automation. The relevant articles are by Dekker and Woods (2002) and Dekker and Hollnagel (2004; henceforth Dekker, Woods, Hollnagel, or DWH). On the basis of a highly selective review of some previous articles published by us (e.g., Parasuraman, Sheridan, & Wickens, 2000), DWH argued that constructs such as SA and trust in automation (complacency) are “folk psychology” concepts (or “folk models”) that are without empirical foundation and lacking scientific status.

In this article, we counter this view by pointing to evidence on the operationalization and empirical basis of these constructs. We also show that their scientific status is as strong as any in cognitive engineering or HF/E. DWH also stated that accident and other applied investigators—as well as, by implication, HF/E researchers who study SA, mental workload, and trust—use these constructs as “explanations” for real-world accidents and incidents in an uncritical and circular way. We agree that some consumers of HF/E research do unfortunately bandy about these constructs in an overgeneralized way occasionally—for example, the military pilot commander who extols the virtue of the “right stuff” and equates it with high SA. However, the leading researchers do not, and they often point to moderating effects and other contributing factors that may limit their ability to account for complex real-world phenomena. DWH appear to have focused on the writings of the applied community, often published in non-peer-reviewed outlets, and neglected to mention the large body of empirical research that constitutes the scientific literature on workload, SA, and trust.

## Are SA,Mental Workload,And Trust in Automation Just “Folk Models”?

DWH took HF/E researchers and practitioners to task for using constructs such as SA, mental workload, and trust in automation. DWH used the term folk model to refer mainly to SA and to complacency. However, complacency has been studied primarily in relation to automation and can be subsumed under the more general area of trust in automation. Specifically, complacency can be related to trust in automation when it is greater than that warranted based on automation reliability or robustness. Furthermore, workload not only is encountered in everyday parlance (by “common folk”) but also is sometimes used uncritically by HF/E consumers—in the manner that DWH pointed out—to “explain” real-world incidents (“the accident resulted from an overloaded operator . . .”). DWH also referred to workload in both articles and stated that high workload could “possibly” be invoked in an accident investigation (Dekker & Hollnagel, 2004, p. 85), thus implying that this concept shares the same characteristics they ascribed to SA and complacency. We therefore take DWH’s critique to apply to each of these three human performance constructs.

One of DWH’s primary objections is that these constructs constitute folk models or “folk psychology.” DWH apparently intended the characterization to be pejorative, with the folk tag meant to show that these constructs lack scientific status. We strongly disagree. But before we bury Caesar, let us praise him.

Folk psychology is a legitimate topic of inquiry. It refers not just to conceptions of mental phenomena that laypeople (“common folk”) possess but also, as philosophers of science have pointed out (e.g., Goldman, 1993), to the examination of the relation between intuitive (or naive) conceptions of the mind and objective charac terizations based on cognitive psychology. Sometimes the intuitive and objective char acterizations coincide, and sometimes they do not, as in the case of “folk physics” (McCloskey, 1983). And certainly, folk psychology is an important starting point for empirically based investigations of many mental phenomena—witness William James’s oft-quoted statement that “everybody knows what attention is” and the subsequent explosion of behavioral and neural research on the mechanisms of attention (Broadbent, 1958; Kramer, Kirlik, & Wiegmann, 2007; Moray, 1969; Parasuraman, 1998; Parasuraman & Davies, 1984; Pashler, 1998; Posner, 2004).

Hence, we view DWH’s indictment of SA, mental workload, and trust as mere folk model concepts not as deprecatory but rather as irrelevant. We argue, as will be subsequently supported by reference to literature reviews covering many hundreds of studies, that these constructs are as well operationalized as any in HF/E research. In fact, we challenge DWH to cite a larger body of supporting empirical evidence for any other modern cognitive engineering construct. This would include their proposed concept of “team play.” By empirical evidence, we mean controlled experimental studies and/or validated computational models and not just subjec tive observations, analytical exercises, or personal opinions.

The supporting evidence we describe includes experimental studies, converging results from different methodologies, and computational modeling. In contrast, the picture that DWH painted is a caricature. Their characterization of these human cognition and performance constructs neglects to mention the many extensive and careful quantification and modeling efforts that have been conducted in the examination of SA, workload, and trust. In the following, we emphasize both the scien tific attributes underlying the three constructs (e.g., their operational definitions, predictions, and falsifiability) as well as their diagnostic value in assisting HF/E practitioners to formulate specific solutions or remedies when any of the three constructs suggests suboptimal human-system interaction. We go on to specifi cally address the concerns of DWH regarding treatments of automation in func tion allocation.

## Situation Awareness

DWH saw little merit in the construct of SA for HF/E research and practice, instead consigning it to the folk model bin. For example, in Dekker and Hollnagel (2004), they stated,

Human factors have [sic] a sizeable stock of concepts that are used to express insights about the functional characteristics of the human mind that underlie complex behavior. . . . The labels refer to concepts that are intuitively meaningful in the sense that everyone associates something with them, so they feel that they understand them. . . . The use and popularity of these labels are evidence of the psychological strategies people turn to when confronted with the daunting complexity of modern technological systems. In scientific terms it can be seen as a tendency to rely on folk models. The concrete reason for exploring these questions is the recent problem of human performance created by high levels of automation on commercial aircraft flight decks. Various provisional explanations for the observed problems have been proposed, including complacency . . . , loss of situation awareness . and loss of effective crew resource management. . . . The problem is that the value of the constructs hinges on their commonsense appeal rather than their substance. Situation awareness lacks a level of detail and so fails to account for a psychological mechanism needed to connect features of the sequence of events to the outcome. . . . The first and most evident characteristic of folk models is that they define their central constructs, the explanandum, by substitution rather than decomposition or reduction. So instead of explaining the central construct by statements that refer to more fundamental and presumably better known explananda, the explanation is made by referring to another phenomenon or construct that itself is in equal need of explanation. (pp. 79–80)

In addition, in reference to SA and complacency, DWH stated that “none of these folk claims have a strong empirical basis” (Dekker & Woods, 2002, p. 242).

We provide these somewhat lengthy quotes so that the reader may understand the nature of DWH’s arguments against the empirical or scientific basis of SA and other constructs. However, the charge that SA is without empirical foundation is itself without foundation. In the wake of the development of the SA construct by Endsley (1995), others also provided important early critiques (Flach, 1995; Sarter & Woods, 1991). But now there is a substantial body of research pointing to its utility (e.g., Banbury & Tremblay, 2004; Durso, Rawson, & Girrotto, 2007; Endsley, 2006; Tsang & Vidulich, 2006; Wickens, 2008; Wickens et al., 2008).

A key feature of SA is that the concept is diagnostic of different human operator states and, therefore, specifically prescriptive as to different HF/E remedies when SA is insufficient (described later). Endsley’s (2006) enduring definition of SA is “the perception of the elements in the environment within a volume of time and space, the comprehension of their meaning, and the projection of their status in the near future” (p. 529). This is a useful definition that remains fairly intact and respected by researchers and practitioners to this day. The prescriptive usefulness of this definition derives as much from what SA is not as it does from what it is.

Most important, SA represents a continuous diagnosis of the state of a dynamic world. As such, there is a “ground truth” against which its accuracy can be assessed (e.g., the objective state of the world or the objective unfolding of events that are predicted). SA is therefore quite distinct from (e.g., it is not) the choice or decision of what action to take as a consequence of the diagnosis. This is because there is no ground truth for choice, because choice must be made on the basis of explicit or implicit values of outcomes, and these values can legitimately vary from person to person. SA, on the other hand, is value free. Accurate choice will depend on good SA, but choice is not the same as SA. This important distinction echoes the partial independence of diagnosis and choice in expected-value models of decision making (Edwards, 1961) and of sensitivity and response bias in signal detection models (Green & Swets, 1966).

A second, closely related, point is that SA is not “performance.” DWH’s failure to note the distinction between a psychological construct and behavioral or performance outcomes may explain why they see little merit in the SA construct. Less understandable is their neglect of the vast body of supporting evidence for the usefulness of SA in cognitive engineering design and training. In this regard, it is important to note that very different sorts of displays, automation levels, or procedures are required for supporting routine performance, whereas other types are needed for supporting SA (Wickens, 2000; Wickens & Prevett, 1995). The typical support in normal circumstances may be very inappropriate when things go wrong, and SA becomes critical. The autopilot will do a wonderful job when the pilot (or driver) is on the anticipated route but may be worthless when emergency rerouting is required or, of course, when it fails. In this case, broad map displays supporting SA are essential.

Third, SA is not general knowledge (long-term memory for facts, procedures, or mental models), which has a relatively long time constant, being acquired (and forgotten) over periods of hours, days, and years. SA, on the other hand, generally applies to more rapidly evolving situations. Long-lasting knowledge supports but is distinct from SA.

This degree of prescriptive specificity of SA makes the construct useful for suggesting different HF/E remedies when SA is found wanting. For example, accidents related to poor SA would be addressed by teaching workers where and how to better seek information (Level 1 SA), integrate it (Level 2), and predict its impli cations (Level 3) rather than, for example, teaching procedures or responses to actions or changing the value structure for different actions. Very different deci sion aids should be provided to physicians if their SA is poor (diagnostic assis tance) than if the treatment they prescribe (decision and choice) is wanting (Garg et al., 2005; Morrow, Wickens, & North, 2006).

Finally, just as the distinction between SA and broader concepts of cognition (choice, knowledge) is useful to HF/E practitioners, the refinement of the three levels within the SA construct is diagnostic. Different methodological techniques can be used to assess Level 1 SA (visual scanning) from those of Levels 2 and 3. Computational models of both Levels 1 and 2 have been developed and validated (Wickens et al., 2008). Breakdowns in noticing (Level 1) invite different classes of solutions (e.g., alerts and attention guidance) than do breakdowns in understanding (Level 2; supported by display integration) and prediction (Level 3; supported by predictive displays).

It is noteworthy that these and other conclusions regarding SA measurement and its implications have been supported by more than a decade of strong empirical research, as described in recent reviews (e.g., Banbury & Tremblay, 2004; Endsley, Bolte, & Jones, 2003; Garland & Endsley, 2000). This research is based on the science of cognition, as well as converging evidence from various measures of behavior, physiology, and subjective ratings, supplemented by computational modeling.

## Mental Workload

There are many parallels between workload and SA. Both are constructs that are distinct from behavior and performance, as well as constructs that are measured by triangulation from physiology, performance, and subjective assessments, coupled with task analysis and computational modeling (Wickens, 2000). Workload, like SA, also generates specific diagnoses with prescriptive HF/E remedies when workload is suboptimal (here we focus on excessive overload, although solutions for underload have also been addressed). At the most general level, mental workload can be described as the relation between the function relating the mental resources demanded by a task and those resources available to be supplied by the human operator. Its history predates that of SA by about two decades, first comprehensively reviewed in Moray (1979).

Assessment of mental workload had a major impact on system design in the decision to downsize the flight deck from three to two crewmembers by eliminating the flight engineer’s position. The problem arose in the context of certification by the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) of new aircraft, such as the DC 9-80 and Boeing 757/767 (Ruggerio & Fadden, 1987). The decision was directly supported by a workload assessment of the two-crew design to ensure that the demands of flight tasks did not exceed the capacities of the two-person crew. The FAA asked one of the current authors (Sheridan) and Robert Simpson at MIT to investigate the possibility of measuring or quantitatively rating mental workload to provide a basis for deciding between three and two pilots. Following a year of riding an airline pilot’s jump seat, Sheridan and Simpson (1979) developed a workload rating scale, analogous to the then well established Cooper-Harper rating scale for handling qualities, that was used to support the two-crew design.

Like SA, the workload concept has considerable value from the perspective of what it is not as much as what it is. In particular, the idea that workload is not performance has led researchers to examine ways of assessing the residual attention available from performing a task, a measure of great importance and one that can be fully dissociated from performance. Two people performing the same task (or two systems used by the same person) can generate identical behavioral and performance outputs, yet one person may have plenty of attention left to allocate to concurrent tasks, whereas the other does not (Vidulich & Wickens, 1986; Yeh & Wickens, 1988).

Furthermore, the solutions or remedies suggested by inadequate performance may not necessarily be the same as those suggested by a diagnosis of excessive workload. For example, performance decrements may result from an inadequate interface. If critical information is displayed in a location where it cannot be accessed or represented in an acronym that cannot be understood, this is not a workload issue, but it certainly degrades performance. A computer mouse with a high gain will create unstable target acquisition—a performance decrement but, again, not originally a workload problem (even though the poor performance may elicit a subjective reaction of high perceived workload).

In contrast, a task environment that requires the operator to hold a large amount of information in working memory while seeking more information or while conversing clearly does describe a workload problem, even if most operators are capable of doing so without making many errors (as long as they are not interrupted by unexpected new demands). A human manual control system with very high display magnification even more clearly demonstrates the dissociation between workload and performance. Such a system could produce exceptionally good tracking performance (low error), but the resulting investment of resources to correct even the smallest of deviations will increase to a great extent the workload that is experienced (Vidulich & Wickens, 1986).

A third example of the dissociation between workload and performance comes from a classic study of air traffic controllers by Sperandio (1971). As the task load on an operator increases, one might presume that performance would decrease and workload increase. However, Sperandio found that as the number of aircraft in their sectors increased, approach controllers tasked with giving landing instructions to aircraft maintained their performance at the same level, even as they experienced greater workload. Sperandio observed that controllers used a variety of adaptive strategies, such as processing fewer aircraft variables and reducing verbal communications, to keep performance stable under high task load. (For a discussion of operator adaptive strategies and workload, see Parasuraman & Hancock, 2001.) Thus, the construct of mental workload is vital in understanding the relation between objective task load and task strategies.

A final example comes from a common daily activity: driving. Workload can be a better predictor of drivers’ future performance than of their current perform ance. One can measure excessively high driver workload (e.g., using objective physiological or secondary task measures) when steering and speed are perfect and the car is properly and accurately following the road. In other words, high workload can be accommodated without immediate consequence for perform ance. At some later point, however, the sustained high workload experienced by the driver may not allow him or her to respond effectively to an unexpected increase in demand; consequently, the car may veer into a ditch.

Correspondingly, as with SA, the workload concept gains utility by the greater definition and refinement of its different elements. For example, the diagnosis of excessive cognitive workload will suggest different remedies (e.g., working memory support) than that of excessive visual-motor workload (e.g., predictive displays).

Finally, over the past half century, the concept of mental workload has been supported by strong science. On one hand, workload can be related to various measures of brain and autonomic system activity (Kramer & Parasuraman, 2007; Parasuraman & Wilson, 2008; Tsang & Wilson, 1997; Wickens & McCarley, 2007). At the same time, converging evidence from performance, physiology, and subjective ratings has validated the construct, generated testable hypotheses, and, most important, provided useful science-based solutions to workers in a variety of endeavors.

## Trust in Automation: The Issue of Complacency

Automated systems have not always been used by human operators in the ways that designers intended (Wiener & Curry, 1980; Woods, 1996). Automation may be used appropriately at times, but instances of misuse and disuse are also prevalent (Parasuraman & Riley, 1997). One of the major factors influencing both misuse and disuse is trust in automation. Lee and Moray (1992) conducted pio neering research showing that an operator’s use of automation to control a simulated manufacturing plant was directly related to his or her momentary trust, which in turn was related to the type and frequency of faults. In a subsequent study, Lee and Moray (1994) showed that operators chose to use automation if their trust in automation exceeded their confidence in their own ability to control the plant but otherwise chose manual control.

A noteworthy aspect of Lee and Moray’s research was their development of a simple mathematical model relating variations in trust in automation and self-confidence to human system performance. Since this work, there has been a large body of research examining the interrelationships between trust and factors such as automation reliability, error type, task difficulty, and other factors (Bisantz & Seong, 2001; Dzindolet, Peterson, Pomranky, Pierce, & Beck, 2003; Dzindolet, Pierce, Beck, Dawe, & Anderson, 2001; Wiegmann, Rich, & Zhang, 2001; see Lee & See, 2004, for a review).

This large body of research has clearly established the importance of trust in the human use of automation. Although there are different models and theories of trust in automation (Cohen, Parasuraman, & Freeman, 1998; Dzindolet et al., 2003; Lee & See, 2004; Madhavan & Wiegmann, 2007), there is broad agreement that the construct not only is worthwhile but also can be used, in conjunction with other factors, to predict human dependence (or not) on automation, as well as linked to objective measures of human performance. Is the trust construct relevant to performance in the real world? We think so. In their critique, DWH argued that human performance, rather than psychological constructs such as SA and complacency, should be used in understanding real-world behavior, including accidents and incidents (Dekker & Hollnagel, 2004, p. 84). Their argument does not hold, because these concepts were operationalized in terms of human performance. A vast amount of human performance literature on trust also provides a sound basis for its operationalization.

Variations in trust are one factor in an operator’s use of automation. Low levels of trust can lead to disuse, as is often the case with automated alarm systems that generate many false alerts (Dixon, Wickens, & McCarley, 2007; Parasuraman, Hancock, & Olofinboba, 1997). Very high levels of trust in automation that are not perfectly reliable can be associated with overreliance and failure to monitor the “raw” information sources that provide input to the automated system. This is the complacency issue. The term is somewhat unfortunate, in that in everyday parlance, complacency tends to suggest willful and ill-advised neglect, whereas the empirical phenomenon of reduced monitoring does not and, in fact, could be considered a rational strategy (Moray & Inagaki, 2000). For historical reasons stemming from its long use in the aviation domain, the term continues to appear.

DWH cited many definitions of complacency (including ones from those whom we would characterize as uncritical HF/E consumers, not HF/E researchers), as if the preponderance of descriptors is sufficient evidence to bring in a guilty verdict on the charge of lacking scientific status. Even in this method of guilt by association, DWH erred. On two occasions (Dekker & Hollnagel, 2004, p. 81), they attributed one proposed definition of complacency—“self-satisfaction which may result in non-vigilance based on an unjustified assumption of satisfactory system state”—to an article written by one of the present authors (Parasuraman,

Molloy, & Singh, 1993). However, the original source (as Parasuraman et al., 1993, pointed out) was Charles Billings, a leading aviation HF/E researcher (Billings, Lauber, Funkhouser, Lyman, & Huff, 1976). The reference by Billings et al. (1976) is a NASA report on the Aviation Reporting Safety System (ASRS).

Even if one ignores DWH’s errors, their claim that the existence of multiple definitions necessarily dooms a construct to scientific purgatory is highly suspect. Look up 10 books on attention, and you are likely to find 10 definitions of attention. But is there any doubt as to the scientific status of the construct of attention?

The scientific basis of complacency and related constructs must be determined empirically. If one goes beyond the inconsistencies in terminology and looks at the empirical findings, one finds numerous well-controlled studies indicating that operator monitoring in systems with imperfect automation can be poor. This is not to say that it is always poor. Parasuraman et al. (1993) first showed that monitoring of the information sources feeding automation was deficient only when operators had other tasks to perform and automation reliability was unchanging. The basic effect has been replicated (Bagheri & Jamieson, 2004; Bailey & Scerbo, 2007; Metzger & Parasuraman, 2005; Molloy & Parasuraman, 1996) as well as modeled computationally (Farrell & Lewandowsky, 2000; Wickens & Dixon, 2007). The phenomenon can be observed not just with college student participants but also with skilled pro fessionals tested in high-fidelity simulators, including pilots (Galster & Parasuraman, 2001) and air traffic controllers (Metzger & Parasuraman, 2005).

The results of the original study by Parasuraman et al. (1993) suggested that complacency reflects a strategy of allocating attention away from the automated task to other concurrent tasks. The allocation strategy itself may result from high trust in the automation. One limitation of the study was that trust was not measured independently; however, this was done in subsequent studies that replicated the basic findings (Bagheri & Jamieson, 2006; Bailey & Scerbo, 2007; Manzey, Bahner, & Hueper, 2006).

Moray and Inagaki (2000) suggested that the attention allocation strategy could be rational and, furthermore, that complacency should be inferred only if the rate of monitoring was below that of an “optimal” observer who was required to attend to many sources of information (the automated task being one such source). Senders (1964) first showed that human observers tasked with monitoring for abnormal readings on multiple dials with different frequencies of changing values (bandwidth) made eye movements to the dials in proportion to bandwidth. This is consistent with the Nyquist theorem (a continuous variable can be repro duced exactly if it is sampled at least at twice the highest frequency component). Moray and Inagaki suggested that a human operator who monitored automation at a lesser rate than the optimal Nyquist frequency was complacent and the one who monitored at a greater rate was skeptical, whereas the one who monitored at the optimal rate was eutactic (or well calibrated).

Moray and Inagaki’s (2000) proposal is an important one. However, the proposal may be difficult to test because the frequency spectrum of many real-world information sources can be complicated to compute and can also make it difficult for the human observer to perceive the different frequencies of change. Moray and Inagaki also made the point that complacency should be evaluated independently of performance with automation. (This reiterates the distinction made previously between a psychological construct such as SA, workload, or complacency and performance as an outcome.) Thus, for example, if complacency reflects allocation of attention to other concurrent tasks, then eye movement recordings should show that operators scan the raw information sources less frequently when using automation than when performing the task manually or less frequently when automation reliability is higher than lower. This is indeed the case (Bagheri & Jamieson, 2004; Metzger & Parasuraman, 2005; Wickens, Dixon, Goh, & Hammer, 2005). Furthermore, if operators are given an explicit tool to uncover the raw information sources (Lorenz, Di Nocera, Rottger, & Parasuraman, 2002), then complacency is indicated if they use the tool less often under automation than under manual control. Manzey et al. (2006) conducted a series of experiments that verify this prediction.

Unfortunately, DWH referred to none of this rich body of empirical work but instead got wrapped up in the issue of folk models and inconsistent definitions of complacency, thereby implying that there is no solid empirical evidence for the construct. They also raised the legitimate issue that accident investigators can use complacency and other human performance constructs in an uncritical way. But here, too, they mistook the messenger for the message and ignored the underlying empirical evidence, as illustrated by the following:

Take as an example an automation-related accident that occurred in 1973, when situation awareness or automation-induced complacency had not yet come into use. The aircraft in question was on approach. . [and] was equipped with a slightly deficient “flight director”. The airplane struck a seawall bounding Boston short of the runway killing all 89 people onboard. . . . The safety board explained how an accumulation of discrepancies, none of which were critical in themselves, had rapidly brought about a high-risk situation without positive flight management. The first officer, who was flying, was preoccupied with the information presented by his flight director systems, to the detriment of his attention to altitude, heading and airspeed control. Today, both automation-induced complacency of the first officer and a loss of situation awareness of the entire crew would most likely be cited under the causes of this crash. . . . These “explanations” (complacency, loss of situation awareness) were obviously not needed in the early 1970s to deal with such accidents. The analysis instead proposed a set of more detailed, more falsifiable, and more traceable assertions that linked features of the situation (such as an accumulation of discrepancies) to measurable or demonstrable aspects of human performance (diversion of attention to the flight director versus other sources of data). (Dekker & Hollnagel, 2004, p. 84)

Note the last statement, in which they mentioned “diversion of attention to the flight director versus other sources of data.” This is the precise finding from the empirical studies of complacency described earlier! Studies using eye movements and other information-sampling techniques do in fact point to diversion of attention away from the primary information sources (Manzey et al., 2006; Metzger & Parasuraman, 2005; Thomas & Wickens, 2004). As to DWH’s statement that explanations should be based on “measurable or demonstrable aspects of human performance,” this is exactly what the studies described earlier did: They measured such behaviors as detection rate and response time and linked these to operational definitions of complacency or Level 1 SA, such as eye movements and use of an information-sampling tool.

## Summary on SA, Workload, and Trust

The studies we have described provide a strong empirical database that establishes a scientific basis for the cognitive engineering constructs of SA, workload, and trust. DWH proposed that these constructs are not well specified in terms of more basic psychological mechanisms. This is a legitimate concern, but the available evidence does not support their view. All three constructs have been linked to information-processing or other psychological processes (for SA, see Durso et al., 2007, and Endsley, 1995; for workload, see Kahneman, 1973, and Wickens, 1984; for trust, see Lee & See, 2004). Furthermore, some of the underlying brain mechanisms have also been identified (for SA, see Wilson, 2000; for workload, see Kramer & Weber, 2000, and Parasuraman & Wilson, 2008; for trust, see Adolphs, 2002, and Krueger et al., 2007). Finally, each construct has been successfully modeled computationally (for SA, see Banbury & Tremblay, 2004, and Wickens et al., 2008; for workload, see Wickens, 2002; for trust, see Cohen et al., 1988, and Gao & Lee, 2006).

These different methodologies provide converging evidence for the scientific viability of these constructs. Of course, this is not to say that there is complete agreement on the underlying mechanisms or that future research may not lead to revisions of the componential structures or modeling of these constructs—that is the nature of scientific progress. But their scientific status seems indisputable.

## Function Allocation and the Design of Automated Systems

The constructs of SA, mental workload, and trust are often invoked in considerations of function allocation and the design of automated systems. DWH (Dekker & Woods, 2002) specifically addressed the function allocation issue, using as a springboard a previous article by the present authors (Parasuraman et al., 2000). A major aspect of their critique is based on their assertion of Parasuraman et al.’s (2000) belief in “fixed human and machine strengths and weaknesses” (Dekker & Woods, 2002, p. 240).

This characterization is stunningly inaccurate. Parasuraman et al. (2000) explic itly stated that machine strengths and weaknesses continue to evolve (whereas human strengths and weaknesses evolve with learning), as shown in the following quotes: “Machines, especially computers, are now capable of carrying out many functions that at one time could only be performed by humans” (p. 286), and “any proposed (automation) taxonomy is likely to be superceded by technological developments in methods for information integration and presentation” (p. 294). Parasuraman et al. (2000) proposed that there was a need for determining experimentally what should and should not be automated, based on cognitive engineering data and other considerations (e.g., cost and ease of system integration). Of course, engineers must be cognizant of the “ballpark” strengths and weaknesses of both humans and machines to practice the art of engineering in performing experimental developments. However, Parasuraman et al. (2000) explicitly stated that these capabilities are not fixed but change over time.

Like many others, we appreciate satire, especially when insightful. But we see little value in demeaning the historically important Fitts (1951) “Men are better at, machines are better at” (MABA-MABA) table by calling it, as DWH did, “abracadabra.” The acronym was coined in the 1980s simply as a mnemonic. Of course, the Fitts list is no longer valid as originally stated, because machines have long since surpassed humans in many of the categories stated by Fitts in 1951.

DWH also asserted that “these large motherhood labels for possible performance decrements know little consensus in the HF community.” They also referred to the “engineer who uses the method,” and, a few lines later, postulated that MABA-MABA methods are “a retread of ancient beliefs in which the human-machine ensemble resembles a linear input output device that takes an impoverished stimulus from the world and converts it into a response by adding meaning through various distinct processing stages along the way” (Dekker & Woods, 2002, p. 241). DWH’s statements reveal a naïveté about how engineering is done, as well as how information-processing models have evolved, the latter perhaps as a result of neg lect or their having read only the older and not modern cognitive psychology literature (Wickens & Carswell, 2007).

First of all, there has never been even a hint that the levels of automation scale represent a “method” (implying that one need only turn a crank and design is done). In fact, Parasuraman et al. (2000) stated, “As a qualitative model our approach is meant to provide a framework for design, not a set of quantitative methods” (p. 294, emphasis added). Sheridan (2002) and Sheridan and Verplank (1979) also consistently maintained that the levels of the automation scale are only a qualitative dimension and that there are many ways to express the spectrum from no to full automation (see also Sheridan & Parasuraman, 2006). Furthermore, as Sheridan and Verplank initially suggested, the scale is surely multidimensional, as developed further in subsequent publications (e.g., Parasuraman et al., 2000).

DWH criticized MABA-MABA lists (and Parasuraman et al.’s [2000] levels and stages of automation approach, by inference) for not including many other human attributes they list—information filtering, scheduling, anticipating, inferring, learning, and others—which “fall by the wayside, misleading the designer who relies on the list” and foster “the idea that new technology can be introduced as a simple substitution of machines for people” (Dekker & Woods, 2002, p. 240). In fact, however, many of these are treated explicitly within the Parasuraman et al. (2000) article (e.g., information filtering, anticipating, inferring). DWH have conjured up a straw man “substitution myth” that was never stated by Parasuraman et al. (2000)—and surely never intended—so that they can knock it down. Throughout their article, Dekker & Woods (2002) stated that Parasuraman et al. (2000) are advocates of “substitution,” whereas no such advocacy is put forth other than to point out the fact that designers do in effect substitute because that is what they do when they adopt automation. But rather than advocating substitution, the point of Parasuraman et al. (2000) was to give designers guidance as to what to consider—not a “method”—on what may be the more harmonious way to substitute.

Certainly computers and automation are replacing human sensory-motor and cognitive tasks of all kinds, and quite successfully. But the “substitution” is far from simple, as DWH suggested that Parasuraman et al. (2000) believe. Currently, for example, major government and industry efforts are under way to reallocate human and machine functions for air traffic control (ATC). The issue has arisen in the context of modernizing the ATC system, which is increasingly ill equipped to handle the projected growth in air traffic over the next decade. Because it has been ascertained empirically that human controllers simply cannot perform the necessary tasks, it is believed that more automation can help. The work on how to revise the human and machine roles and develop the automation is a gigantic research, development, and human-in-the-loop simulation effort, not one of mere substitution.

The reader should also note that the original Parasuraman et al. (2000) article was a consequence of recommendations from a three-year study (1994–1997) by the National Research Council panel on human factors in the current and future ATC system. All three of the present authors served on that panel, and it is gratify ing to note that the recommendations for automation design made in the panel report (Wickens, Mavor, Parasuraman, & McGee, 1998) appear to have been followed by the FAA (Ahlstrom, Longo, & Truitt, 2005).

Dekker and Woods (2002, p. 243) concluded in a section titled “Progress Toward Better Team Play” that “automation is not who has control over what and how much? It is how do we get along together?” They went on to state well-established HF/E principles as a remedy for better team play. We do not refute the latter but note that the three prescriptions for better team play that they propose—highlighting changes, displaying future projections, and visually integrating information (ecological displays)—are not new but instead are time-honored principles that were validated many years ago in HF/E research. Furthermore, all of these recommendations are explicitly incorporated within Stages 1 and 2 of the automation taxonomy provided by Parasuraman et al. (2000).

## Discussion

We have provided only a partial account of the large body of empirical research examining the constructs of SA, mental workload, and trust. We cannot burden the reader with a comprehensive review, but we can confidently state that empiri cal studies supporting the SA, workload, and trust constructs are numerous (in the hundreds). Quantity is not everything, of course, but the vast majority of the empirical research is of high quality. The studies meet the most widely accepted standards of scientific rigor and do not represent mere folk psychology (even if that was a drawback, which, as we have argued, it is not). The product of quantity and quality is a large number.

This scientific literature on SA, workload, and trust has done precisely what DWH claim it has not done—that is, it has operationalized these constructs in terms of measurable confluences (triangulation) of performance, environmental inputs (e.g., low target/nontarget ratios, heavy task demands), and, where applicable, subjec tive or physiological measures to define precise and useful concepts, supplemented by computational models of the constructs. As a consequence of this science, these concepts become meaningful as contributors to performance but are far from being equivalent to performance (as reflected in the concept of dissociation). In contrast, in discussing team play and other constructs, DWH made wide generalizations without citing scientific literature to back up their assertions. We conclude that SA, mental workload, and trust in automation are viable, empirically supported constructs that go beyond folk psychology and are useful in understanding human-system performance in complex work environments.

A fundamental flaw in DWH’s argument in favor of treating these constructs as folk models is their blame-by-association approach. Because some consumers (e.g., accident investigators, program managers) of empirically driven HF/E research on SA, mental workload, and trust have too casually bandied about these terms, DWH argued that the constructs therefore represent folk models unworthy of scientific status. If one were to adopt DWH’s approach, virtually no area of science would be left unscathed, given the current state of popular media. There is no doubt that some consumers do tend to overgeneralize these terms. But the science behind them and the scientific publications do not support that overgeneralization.

Lest the reader think that our response to DWH is too defensive, we wish to point out that we see some value in DWH’s team play approach but think of it as comple mentary to ours rather than antagonistic. In particular, it is generally recognized that in designing human-centered automation, one must consider two meta-components: the functionality and the interface (see Riley, 2000, for an excellent discussion of this distinction). Parasuraman et al.’s (2000) approach very much addresses functionality: what automation should actually do and how aggressively it should do it. The team play approach that DWH advocated, however, at least in part, addresses the interface between the automated system and the human operator/supervisor.

DWH’s three prescriptions pertain to how information about the automation and the processes it controls is displayed to the operator, rather than to the functionality of the automation. As stated previously, we agree with the importance of DWH’s three well-supported HF/E principles, but they apply primarily to interface design, not automation functionality design. Thus, the two approaches are nicely comple mentary. We also see in DWH’s team play concept the invocation of a third metacomponent, which is increasingly being examined in contemporary research: the degree, level, and kind of automation flexibility or coordination that is enabled, an issue often examined within the framework of adaptive automation (e.g., see Miller & Parasuraman, 2007).

A final argument raised by DWH against the scientific status of SA, workload, and trust—their attempt to put the last nail in the coffin—is that these constructs, being folk models, are immune to falsification. Somewhat amazingly, they put these constructs in the same camp as Freudian psychoanalytic “theories” of toilet train ing of infants (Dekker & Hollnagel, 2004, p. 81). This is another example of their blame-by-association method. We have two responses.

First, a fundamental principle of science and the scientific method is that it continually seeks to improve itself by falsifying current (accepted) ideas (explana tions, predictions) based on evidence. This is what distinguishes science from metaphor (art, music, poetry, religion, beauty, love, etc., or pseudoscientific theo ries such as Freudian psychoanalysis, which are not falsifiable), based on rational rules for what constitutes credible and acceptable evidence (e.g., there is no evidence for God). However, constructs such as SA, mental workload, and trust are falsifiable in terms of their usefulness in prediction. (Because a construct is not a statement of fact, falsifiability of the construct itself is a meaningless notion.) For example, that a particular interface to System A leads to some measurable degree of SA, whereas System B is associated with less SA, is quite falsifiable.

Second, Popper’s (1959) well-known criteria for falsification may be less applicable to theory development in understanding human interaction with complex systems (and there are many objections to Popper’s views in the physical sciences as well). In our view, cognitive engineering should not be unduly concerned with the ability to falsify a theory; rather, it should seek to establish the contextual limitations in which a theory or principle successfully predicts performance and makes testable recommendations for the improved design of systems. Theories should not be held to the standard of being proven “right or wrong” but, rather, in cognitive engineering and HF/E, to the standards of being able to specify the constraints in which they account for more or less variance in human-system performance. By these and other criteria, SA, mental workload, and trust remain highly valuable constructs for understanding and predicting human-system performance.
