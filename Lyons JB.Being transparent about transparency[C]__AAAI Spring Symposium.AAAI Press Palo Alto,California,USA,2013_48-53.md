# Being transparent about pay transparency

Chris Kirby

## Abstract

Purpose – Conceptually, pay transparency is in its infancy. But it is highly likely to stay – and organisations need to get their head around it before they are caught on the back foot. This study will advise organisations on their approach to total compensation and performance.

Design/methodology/approach – This study specifically focused on the following: There are many positives, not least saving time and effort, plus creating synchronicity between expectations from the moment a role is advertised to the moment someone leaves. The difficulty is the current cost of living crisis – for some employees, full pay transparency will show that they are already well paid, whereas others will feel frustrated with their pay, as well as any pay freezes. Transparency also needs to go deeper than the pay itself and should factor total compensation and ‘‘life’’ at the organisation. It needs to show flexible working, supporting employees as people – the employee value proposition.

Findings – Pay transparency is harder to unpick and reshape than it is to implement from the start. So, it is more common for start-ups than those with decades of legacy processes across countries, sites, entities or linked, perhaps, to length of service. However, that is out of line with the expectations of many in the modern workforce. Many organisations are being challenged, plus there is a risk that they are seemingly hiding something. There are, too, significant variations between industries, which creates complexities.

Originality/value – This paper is written exclusively for strategic HR review.

Keywords Change, Technology, Transactional, Strategy, Transformation

Paper type Viewpoint

T here was a time – not so long ago – that the question of pay transparency was abinary choice: “should we, or shouldn’t we?” Not so now.

Employee expectations have changed significantly over the years while discussions around transparency and openness are much more nuanced. The modern workforce is made up of a very different set of values and priorities than previous generations and the technology revolution of the last couple of decades has created a workforce who are wired to think differently.

Employees – as consumers – are much more aware, they need to know their employer and brand is genuine in its values – not just by words, but by deeds.

This too, is a cultural element because when it comes to salary, it still feels uncomfortable for large sections of the workforce to be open about something that traditionally has a stigma attached to it.

Yet conversely, the current workforce has been raised in a digital world, where their whole lives are online for everyone to see. There is the expectation that everything is open and transparent, with information available all the time. Public discussions in the mainstream media and on social media channels have enabled people to share views and experiences about remuneration.

Yet therein lies the difference. With social media, you can pick and choose what you share. This is not possible with pay transparency in an organisational setting; you cannot share only the positives.

Pay gap reporting of gender and/or ethnicity is a case in point. Gender pay data is now mandatory for organisations with 250 employees or more, whereas ethnicity pay data is not yet mandatory but is a growing area of focus. Both, however, shine a light on pay disparities within an organisation’s workforce, regardless if that light reflects well or badly on the organisation.

The current cost of living is creating an added layer of complexity. Although pay transparency may demonstrate to some employees that they are already well paid, others may feel frustrated and resentful when they see how their salary compares to others. Pay freezes too, create issues.

When it comes to recruitment, people want to know their expected salaries are competitive and pitched at the market rate. Lack of clarity around remuneration will only put applicants off, turning instead to competitors who do offer full transparency.

Given the risks of organisations denying themselves this competitive advantage, it is astonishing that clarity and transparency in UK job advertisements has reached a sevenyear low (Hunter, 2023). Just over half of 80 million job advertisements posted between 2016 and 2023 and analysed by job search engine Adzuna provided any clarity over salary and remuneration packages. Indeed, the industries struggling the most with labour shortages – such as retail and hospitality – were found to be the most secretive about pay.

Together, this is all at odds with the current zeitgeist, where employees demand pay transparency and need remuneration and reward clarity more than ever.

It is also an important tool in demonstrating organisational values to employees. Organisations with a culture of pay transparency are likely to be perceived as forwardthinking, with a commitment to fairness and openness. According to Talent.com, 78% of employees have a positive attitude towards pay transparency for instance, whereas 74% believe it creates a fairer workplace (Percy, 2022; Talent.com, 2002).

So while conceptually at least, pay transparency may still be in its infancy with some organisations choosing to remain protective over their pay structures, pay transparency is not only here to stay, it is likely to gain momentum. As much as it may feel uncomfortable, it is imperative that organisations get to grips with pay transparency – what it is, what it means, what it looks like.

## Looking beyond pay

Organisations considering implementing full pay transparency need to look at the issue from every possible angle. The political: how can transparency address pay gaps? The practical: how can organisations implement full pay transparency policies and bring people on board? The psychological: why are conversations about pay still taboo? The potential employee-relations fall-out: how do you manage conflict and resentment resulting from pay transparency policies? And accountability: how do you measure the impact of pay transparency? How do you ensure it leads to genuine pay parity across the organisation?

In essence, transparency needs to go deeper than pay itself. Not everyone is motivated by money or progression. An extra £5,000 a year for one person may actually carry the same value as an extra five days’ leave for another or an extra 10% into the workplace pension scheme.

Pay transparency needs to show the full picture of every role, including remuneration and the opportunity and method of flexing. It is about supporting employees as people and taking the holistic approach.

In this way, full pay transparency creates synchronicity between expectation from the moment a job is advertised to the point at which someone leaves.

## Pay justification

Anecdotally, there is often a lot of understandable nervousness around remuneration differences for people at the same level, performing the same role. But there should be reasons for this: length of service or high performance over several years, for example.

Sharing the method for determining pay can help solve this puzzle – or should do. This is where full pay transparency comes in. Being transparent on pay also means being transparent on methods, including total rewards and benefits, to highlight how employees with differing priorities are still treated fairly.

And if there are not justifiable reasons for remuneration differences, pay transparency serves to highlight the work that still needs to be done on pay strategies and processes. And most, if not all organisations, embarking on a pay transparency journey will find they have work still to be done.

## Easier to implement than reshape

For start-ups, the journey towards pay transparency will be relatively straightforward because it is always easier to implement right from the beginning than to unpick and reshape existing processes. Not so for established market leaders with legacy systems, ad hoc arrangements and different frameworks across regions, countries, sites and/or business units. Many of these businesses are acquisition-based, regularly bringing in new terms and conditions, whereas the workforce itself will have a diversity of length of service, benefit eligibility and likely to be geographically diverse.

Business leaders in these organisations will have quite a challenge on their hands. A challenge that must nevertheless be addressed and sooner rather than later – or run the risk of accusations of secrecy. After all, what reason is there for organisations to be protective of their pay strategies?

## Full pay transparency

So with all this in mind, what does genuine full pay transparency look like?

Here is what it is not: being transparent for 90% of the workforce while protecting a certain business unit or level. Being clear with salary on a job advertisement, but vague about pay structures at an organisation-wide level. Saying a pay transparency policy is in operation but pay data is not available.

While it is absolutely true that pay transparency looks different for every organisation and for every individual, it is also true that at its core, pay transparency has to be about trust, good communication and clear strategy.

An IT company, for instance, is likely to have robust bandings in place already and operate fairly rigidly when it comes to paying its workforce incrementally, whereas a sales organisation is more likely to be heavily based on individuals’ performance, so pay is more likely to be defined by the value they can bring the business.

## Aligning pay transparency strategy with values

But organisations operating a genuine pay transparency culture have one thing at least in common: a clear communication strategy that not only reports and shares pay structures but also the method of determining pay and does so publicly. Some organisations may want to go a step further and share the pay for every role and the justification behind it, but this may not be the right approach for every organisation.

Ultimately, at its core, pay transparency is about demonstrating a fair and justified approach to pay. It is also about understanding the audience, too.

Knowing what employees feel about pay transparency, the reasons why and what they feel about their own pay and what they, as individuals value the most are all important starting points.

It is also particularly important to introduce pay transparency in line with organisational values. Pay transparency is an enabler of values and should be implemented as such. It is much easier to bring the workforce along on the journey if they recognise it is part of organisational culture – one that they can support and feel part of – than what may feel at odds with company culture. It is vital then that pay transparency aligns with – rather than contradict – company values.

Will introducing a culture of full pay transparency cause issues among the workforce? Yes. Will these issues be a challenge to address and work on? Also, yes. But it is important to recognise there will be tough decisions and conversations emerging from any strategic change of direction as well as a cost associated with rationalising and addressing some of the imbalances which crop up as a result.

The journey towards full pay transparency is not a quick overnight trip – it is a process, likely to create a myriad of challenges and concerns. But from the point of view of talent attraction, competitor advantage and crucially, building employee trust, it is one that is wholly worth pursuing.
